# PART I — HISTORICAL AND SYSTEMIC CONTEXT

## Poverty, Deprivation, Institutionalization and Psychiatric Stability

### 1. Purpose and Scope of This Historical Section

This section is provided primarily as historical and systemic context. It is not intended to transform every historical experience described below into a separate allegation requiring adjudication, nor is every criticism of Ontario's psychiatric system advanced as an independent claim for relief.

The context nevertheless matters.

The events at issue did not arise in a vacuum. They occurred within a psychiatric system that possesses extraordinary power over persons diagnosed with serious mental illness: the power to assess them, characterize their behaviour, record conclusions about their mental state, substantially influence how police and other institutions respond to them, and, under circumstances prescribed by law, participate in processes resulting in involuntary confinement and treatment.

My criticism of that system therefore forms part of the background necessary to understand my relationship with CAMH, my concern about the relationship between CAMH and the Toronto Police Service, and the significance I attach to apparently ordinary disputes about credibility, independence, work, behaviour and psychiatric stability.

The objective is twofold, and the objectives are parallel rather than contradictory.

I seek appropriate relief concerning the matters properly before the Tribunal.

I also intend to describe, within the rules and proper scope of these proceedings, the institutional system in which those events occurred.

The latter should not be discarded merely because some experiences that explain the system predate the period directly at issue. Historical evidence can remain important to understanding a contemporary institutional relationship without every historical event itself becoming a separate claim.

---

## 2. The Cycle of Disability, Poverty and Psychiatric Crisis

A central criticism concerns the relationship between disability income, poverty, food security, housing security and psychiatric stability.

Ontario Disability Support Program recipients may already begin from a position of substantial vulnerability. A person who cannot reliably afford both adequate housing and adequate food is not merely experiencing an accounting problem. Those material conditions affect the circumstances under which that person is expected to remain psychiatrically stable.

This criticism is directed expressly at the policy choices of Premier Doug Ford's Ontario government insofar as those choices determine or perpetuate inadequate disability assistance.

The argument must be understood precisely.

I am **not** arguing that starvation creates schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, or another underlying psychiatric disease.

I have a diagnosed psychiatric illness. The argument here does not deny the existence of serious mental illness and does not propose hunger as its original cause.

The proposition is considerably simpler:

**Food is a prerequisite of human functioning. Deprive a human being of adequate food for sufficiently long and that human being will deteriorate.**

The precise breaking point varies from person to person. The underlying principle does not.

Hunger causes suffering. Prolonged and severe deprivation can produce weakness, irritability, desperation, impaired concentration, emotional dysregulation and increasingly abnormal behaviour. A human being possesses no infinite reserve against starvation.

This principle applies to people with psychiatric illnesses and people without them.

There is therefore no contradiction between saying that a person has an underlying psychiatric disorder and saying that hunger, homelessness, exhaustion, fear, isolation or other severe environmental stressors can worsen that person's condition. The original diagnosis and the subsequent deterioration are different questions.

A person with serious mental illness who has been stabilized and discharged does not cease being biologically human upon leaving the hospital. That person requires food, shelter, sleep, safety and the ordinary material foundations of life. If those foundations are removed, deterioration is not mysterious.

It is predictable.

---

## 3. Hunger, Suffering and the Human Breaking Point

The point about food must not be misunderstood as a theory concerning the origin of psychiatric disease.

Take psychiatric diagnosis out of the equation entirely.

Take an otherwise psychologically healthy human being and subject that person to sufficiently severe and prolonged deprivation.

Eventually the person suffers.

Eventually the person reaches a breaking point.

A starving person may cry.

A suffering person may scream.

A desperate person who repeatedly asks for food and receives none may begin shouting for somebody to respond.

Someone who was calm at the beginning of the ordeal may no longer be calm after prolonged deprivation.

The exact hour at which this occurs is irrelevant. Different human beings have different physical reserves and different tolerances for suffering. What matters is the elementary proposition that human beings deteriorate when deprived of the necessities required to sustain them.

That proposition becomes especially important when the resulting behaviour occurs inside a psychiatric institution.

---

## 4. When Ordinary Human Distress Becomes Psychiatric Evidence

A psychiatric environment creates an unusual interpretive problem.

Behaviour occurring inside such an institution is observed through a psychiatric lens. A patient may already possess a serious diagnosis. Staff are charged with observing behaviour. Observations enter clinical records. Those records may subsequently influence decisions concerning treatment, liberty and future assessments.

But a psychiatric patient remains capable of ordinary human reactions.

A person can simultaneously have a psychiatric disorder **and be legitimately angry**.

A person can simultaneously have a psychiatric disorder **and be frightened by confinement**.

A person can simultaneously have a psychiatric disorder **and become irritable because he is hungry**.

A person can simultaneously have a psychiatric disorder **and react strongly to assault, humiliation, sleep deprivation, isolation or prolonged uncertainty**.

Consequently, an institution must remain capable of distinguishing symptoms attributable to psychiatric pathology from behaviour produced, aggravated or materially influenced by circumstances surrounding the patient.

Otherwise a dangerous circularity becomes possible:

**institutional condition → distress → distressed behaviour → documentation of distressed behaviour → distressed behaviour interpreted as further evidence of psychiatric instability.**

The institution may then treat as evidence of the patient's disorder behaviour that the institutional environment itself helped create.

That is not a minor semantic distinction.

When the institution possesses substantial authority over the patient's liberty, it can become enormously consequential.

---

## 5. My Experience of Confinement and Escalating Distress

My historical experiences of psychiatric confinement illustrate this problem.

I have experienced situations in which I believed I had legitimate reasons to object to what was happening to me. Yet once a person is understood primarily as a psychiatric patient, forceful objection can itself become susceptible to psychiatric interpretation.

The underlying message—

**I am angry because I believe what is happening to me is wrong**—

can disappear.

What remains in the institutional description may instead become:

**the patient is angry, agitated, yelling or behaving irrationally.**

My experience of isolation made this problem particularly stark.

I describe being placed alone in a cell-like isolation environment. Initially, I could wait calmly.

Time passed.

I became hungry.

I requested food.

As the isolation and hunger continued, my condition deteriorated. Eventually, obtaining attention required increasingly forceful communication.

The institutional observation emerging at the end of such a sequence can be technically accurate and profoundly incomplete:

**the patient was yelling and screaming.**

But that description, standing alone, destroys the chronology that gives the behaviour meaning.

It removes:

**The patient waited.**

**The patient became hungry.**

**The patient requested food.**

**The request was not satisfied.**

**The deprivation continued.**

**The patient became increasingly distressed.**

**The patient eventually yelled.**

One account merely describes behaviour.

The other explains behaviour.

## Psychiatric assessment cannot fairly collapse the second into the first. The chronology underlying this criticism appears in my original account of the isolation experience.

## 6. Patients Are Not Interchangeable

My criticism extends beyond isolation.

**Sharing a psychiatric diagnosis does not make patients clinically, behaviourally or socially interchangeable.**

People receiving psychiatric care can differ enormously in cognitive functioning, independence, behavioural presentation, propensity toward violence, communication ability, treatment requirements and capacity for independent life.

Those differences matter.

During my institutionalizations I was confined alongside people whom I experienced as profoundly unstable, aggressive and dangerous.

I was subjected to threats, harassment, humiliation, physical aggression and sustained behaviour that I experienced as terrorizing.

Whatever treatment those individuals themselves required, **I should not have been required to absorb their violence and intimidation as a condition of receiving treatment for my own illness.**

I am not similarly situated to another patient merely because both of us possess psychiatric diagnoses.

A comparatively high-functioning, independently functioning and non-violent patient should not automatically be confined in close quarters with patients exhibiting persistent violent, threatening or severely disruptive behaviour.

My objection is not theoretical.

I was the person exposed to it.

From my perspective, forcing me into that environment was not merely treatment conducted under unpleasant circumstances. **The environment itself became an additional source of danger, fear and psychiatric destabilization imposed upon me while I was confined and unable simply to leave.**

The distinction between those patients and me therefore matters.

My diagnosis does not erase my individual functioning, behaviour, history or risk profile.

“Psychiatric patient” cannot become a sufficient classification for placing radically different people together and expecting a comparatively non-violent patient to endure whatever follows.

Someone who has never experienced a locked psychiatric ward may possess a sanitized conception of what involuntary confinement among severely unstable and sometimes violent strangers actually entails.

My historical experience was not sanitized.

I experienced those conditions as dangerous.

I experienced some of the people around me as dangerous.

I was terrorized.

And I reject the proposition that sharing a broad category of psychiatric illness made it appropriate to subject me to that danger.

---

## 7. Confinement Is Itself a Serious Human Event

The length of psychiatric confinement should not be trivialized merely because the confinement occurs inside a hospital rather than a correctional institution.

Weeks constitute a substantial portion of a person's life.

During confinement, ordinary autonomy may be dramatically reduced. Movement is restricted. Privacy is diminished. Daily routines are imposed externally. Access to personal property and ordinary activities may be restricted. Communication with the outside world can become more difficult. Boredom can become profound.

For someone accustomed to working, creating, communicating freely and organizing his own day, that loss of autonomy is not an abstraction.

It is lived hour after hour.

The therapeutic purpose of psychiatric hospitalization does not make those consequences unreal.

Nor does acknowledging those consequences require denying that hospitalization can sometimes be medically necessary.

Both propositions can be true:

**psychiatric hospitalization may sometimes be necessary;**

and

**psychiatric hospitalization, particularly involuntary hospitalization, can itself be traumatic, destabilizing and profoundly disruptive.**

A serious psychiatric system should be capable of confronting both propositions simultaneously.

---

## 8. Food Inside and Outside the Institution

Food appears at two different points in this systemic criticism.

Outside the institution, inadequate disability income can contribute to food insecurity and instability.

Inside the institution, control over food becomes part of an environment in which the patient has substantially reduced autonomy.

The significance is not that every unpleasant meal constitutes mistreatment.

The significance is that someone deprived of control over where he lives, when he leaves, what he does and many aspects of his daily routine becomes unusually dependent upon the institution to meet basic needs adequately.

Food is among the most basic of those needs.

When a patient's distress is being clinically observed, inadequate access to food cannot intelligently be detached from subsequent behaviour.

Again, context matters.

---

## 9. The Economic Contradiction: A Prosperous Apparatus Surrounding an Impoverished Patient

There is a further systemic contradiction that must be stated more forcefully.

The criticism is **not** that CAMH employees should themselves be impoverished.

Doctors should be paid for their expertise.

Nurses should be paid for their work.

Support staff should receive decent wages.

The criticism is what the economic arrangement looks like from the other side of the desk.

**The result is a relationship I regard as parasitic.**

The patient can enter a publicly funded mental-health institution and encounter professionals who are secure, well-fed and comparatively affluent, whose livelihoods are funded by a system ostensibly organized around the patient's welfare.

The patient himself can leave that encounter with an annual disability income insufficient to secure both adequate housing and adequate food.

The institution is sustained.

Its employees are paid.

The professional class surrounding psychiatric disability can build careers, acquire property and live ordinary middle-class lives.

Meanwhile, the supposed beneficiary can deteriorate into hunger, homelessness, isolation, depression, fear and renewed psychiatric crisis.

This disparity is especially stark from the perspective of someone surviving on disability income. An income that may appear merely ordinary when viewed from within the professional middle class can represent extraordinary financial security when compared with the resources available to an ODSP recipient.

The patient sees the contrast every time he enters the institution.

One side of the desk is professionally employed, fed, clothed and economically secure.

The other side may be trying to determine whether this month's inadequate resources should be consumed by shelter, food, medication-related necessities, transportation and the countless other requirements of remaining alive and functional.

This is not merely an aesthetic contrast between rich and poor.

**The poverty of the patient can itself become part of the mechanism producing the next psychiatric crisis.**

---

## 10. The Ford Government and the Architecture of the System

Responsibility for creating this economic arrangement rests substantially with the Government of Ontario and, during the period relevant to this criticism, the government of Premier Doug Ford insofar as provincial policy determines the adequacy of disability assistance and the allocation of public resources.

But identifying the architect does not make the resulting institutional relationship benign.

CAMH and its personnel remain participants in a system in which substantial public resources circulate around the treatment and administration of psychiatric disability while dramatically fewer resources may reach the disabled person himself.

Calling that relationship **“parasitic”** is deliberately provocative.

It identifies the contradiction I intend to place before the Tribunal:

**an elaborate and comparatively prosperous apparatus can exist because people like me require assistance while the people requiring that assistance remain desperately poor.**

The Ford government's responsibility for disability policy does not require blaming an individual CAMH nurse or receptionist for setting ODSP rates.

But neither does the existence of a government policy maker erase the institutional reality experienced by the patient.

The patient does not experience government ministries as isolated boxes on an organizational chart.

The patient experiences a system.

He sees substantial resources available once he becomes a psychiatric problem requiring professional management.

He sees dramatically fewer resources available when he needs food, shelter and ordinary stability in order to remain well outside the institution.

That is the contradiction.

---

## 11. The Parasitic Cycle

This economic contradiction becomes still more troubling when viewed across the entire cycle.

Imagine a person with serious mental illness who becomes psychiatrically unstable while homeless or severely impoverished.

The person enters hospital.

The immediate crisis is treated.

Public resources become available.

Professionals are paid.

A bed exists.

A building exists.

Security exists.

Clinical infrastructure exists.

Food exists.

Medication exists.

The patient stabilizes.

The patient is discharged.

Then the extraordinary concentration of institutional resources surrounding that patient recedes.

The person returns to the economic conditions available outside the institution.

If those conditions do not permit reliable access to housing, nutrition, safety and stability, deterioration may begin again.

Another crisis may follow.

Police or emergency services may become involved.

Another hospitalization may follow.

Again, substantial institutional resources become available.

Another stabilization.

Another discharge.

And the cycle repeats:

**poverty → deprivation and instability → deterioration → crisis → police/emergency intervention → institutionalization → expensive professional management → stabilization → discharge into poverty → renewed deterioration.**

The original version of this historical argument describes precisely this repeated movement between deprivation, hospitalization, stabilization and return to the conditions preceding the crisis.

This is why merely saying that Ontario “funds crisis instead of prevention” does not fully capture my criticism.

My criticism is darker.

**The system can impoverish psychiatric patients outside the institution, concentrate severely destabilized people together inside the institution, expose individual patients to the consequences of one another's deterioration, and then professionally administer the resulting dysfunction at considerable public expense.**

An entire professional apparatus can remain economically healthy while the human beings whose suffering justifies that apparatus remain profoundly economically unhealthy.

That is what I mean by a parasitic relationship.

It does not mean every person employed by CAMH intends to exploit patients.

It describes the structure and its result.

---

## 12. Treatment Resistance and the Extraordinary Power of Psychiatry

My historical experience also exposed me to the more extreme possibilities contained within psychiatric medicine.

During one hospitalization, I encountered another patient who told me that he was undergoing a procedure involving his brain in connection with treatment resistance.

I cannot independently establish from that encounter precisely what medical procedure he underwent, what his diagnosis was, or the clinical reasoning supporting it.

I therefore do not present my understanding of another patient's procedure as established medical fact.

The encounter nevertheless affected me.

It confronted me with the reality that psychiatric medicine can, in exceptional circumstances and subject to legal and medical requirements, extend far beyond ordinary conversation and medication.

Modern psychosurgical procedures exist. Their existence is not folklore.

That reality matters to the institutional context because psychiatric patients interact with a system possessing exceptionally consequential forms of authority and treatment.

The appropriate conclusion is not that every psychiatrist threatens every patient with extreme intervention.

It is that **the power imbalance inherent in serious psychiatric treatment is real**, and consequently safeguards surrounding autonomy, consent, credibility, individualized assessment and humane treatment deserve exceptional attention.

---

## 13. Why ODSP Is Psychiatric Policy Too

The adequacy of ODSP cannot therefore be treated as wholly separate from psychiatric policy.

If Ontario accepts that some citizens are unable, by reason of disability, to obtain sufficient income through ordinary employment, the amount provided to those citizens has consequences.

If that amount is insufficient to maintain basic material stability, the resulting deprivation does not disappear merely because governmental responsibility has been administratively divided between different ministries and institutions.

The psychiatric system encounters the person after deterioration.

The hospital stabilizes the person.

The disability system determines the material resources available after discharge.

Police and emergency services may encounter the person during subsequent crises.

These institutions possess different statutory mandates.

**The human being passes through all of them.**

From the patient's perspective, the bureaucratic boundaries disappear.

There is simply a cycle.

Accordingly, Premier Doug Ford and the Government of Ontario properly form part of the political and systemic criticism advanced here where provincial policy concerning disability assistance is being discussed.

The proposition is not that CAMH should receive inadequate funding because its patients are poor.

The proposition is the opposite:

**A government capable of financing an enormous professional apparatus for psychiatric crisis should be capable of ensuring that disabled people have realistic access to the food and shelter required for ordinary human stability.**

It is perverse to accept extreme deprivation as an ordinary condition of disabled life and then spend enormously greater resources managing the consequences after the person deteriorates.

---

## 14. Why This History Matters to the Present Dispute

This history explains why contemporary interactions with CAMH cannot be understood by treating CAMH merely as an ordinary service provider.

My concern about credibility, psychiatric characterization, work, independence and the involvement of police exists against a history in which psychiatric characterization has had substantial consequences for my autonomy.

That history affects the significance of present events.

It also explains why evidence of functioning matters to me.

Work is not offered as magical proof that psychiatric illness does not exist.

A person with serious mental illness can work.

A person with serious mental illness can create.

A person with serious mental illness can operate sophisticated technology.

None of those propositions erases a diagnosis.

But sustained work can nevertheless constitute relevant evidence about **functioning**.

Long-term planning, sustained concentration, technical problem-solving, maintaining complicated projects, producing creative work, communicating coherently about those projects, operating infrastructure and assuming continuing responsibilities can reveal information about what a person is actually capable of doing.

The appropriate question is therefore not:

**“Does this work prove that the patient has no psychiatric illness?”**

It plainly does not.

The appropriate question is:

**“If functioning is clinically relevant, why should substantial, objectively observable evidence of functioning receive no consideration merely because that functioning occurs through creative or technical work?”**

That distinction becomes important in the contemporary portions of this submission.

---

## 15. Institutional Power Requires Institutional Restraint

Psychiatric institutions deal with people at moments when those people may possess unusually little power.

Their words can be characterized as symptoms.

Their anger can be characterized as agitation.

Their resistance can be characterized as illness.

Their desperation can be recorded without its surrounding circumstances.

Police may become involved.

Liberty may be restricted.

Treatment decisions may follow.

None of this means psychiatric medicine is illegitimate.

It means that **the greater the institution's power over an individual, the greater the importance of accuracy, context, proportionality and individualized treatment.**

A psychiatric diagnosis must never become an interpretive shortcut through which every disagreement proves illness, every protest proves instability, every emotional reaction proves pathology, and contrary evidence of functioning becomes invisible.

Nor should psychiatric diagnosis erase meaningful differences between patients.

I should be assessed according to **my behaviour, my functioning, my history and my actual level of risk**.

I should not be treated as interchangeable with a violent or profoundly unstable stranger merely because both of our medical records contain psychiatric diagnoses.

And I should not be forced to endure terrorization and violence from other patients as though exposure to those conditions were an inherent and unavoidable component of psychiatric treatment.

---

## 16. Conclusion: The System Surrounding the Incident

The historical experiences described here are not included simply because they were painful.

They establish the perspective from which the present dispute must be understood.

The issue is larger than a single unpleasant interaction.

It concerns a system in which poverty, disability assistance, psychiatric treatment, involuntary confinement, police involvement and social marginalization can intersect in the life of the same person.

A government may regard disability benefits as one policy file.

A hospital may regard psychiatric stabilization as another.

Police may regard crisis intervention as another.

But the patient experiences all three as one continuous life.

And there is a profound contradiction at the centre of that life.

**The state can surround psychiatric disability with expensive institutions, professional salaries, physicians, nurses, administrators, security, police intervention and emergency infrastructure while leaving the disabled person himself without sufficient economic resources to reliably secure the ordinary necessities of human stability.**

The machinery can prosper while the patient deteriorates.

The machinery can become most resource-rich precisely when the patient reaches his lowest point.

Then the machinery stabilizes him and returns him to poverty.

If deprivation contributes to another deterioration, the machinery returns.

That is the systemic cycle I intend to criticize.

It is why the comparative prosperity of the institutional apparatus is relevant.

It is why the poverty of the patient is relevant.

It is why food is relevant.

It is why confinement conditions are relevant.

It is why the differences between individual psychiatric patients are relevant.

And it is why the institutional interpretation of behaviour is relevant.

This is not an invitation to adjudicate every historical grievance.

It is an invitation to understand the system before judging what happened within it.

**The patient is not merely the object upon which the mental-health system operates. The patient is the person the system exists to serve. A system that preserves the prosperity and stability of the machinery while repeatedly returning its supposed beneficiary to deprivation, instability and crisis deserves more than administrative acceptance. It deserves scrutiny.**
