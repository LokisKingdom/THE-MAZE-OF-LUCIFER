CITATIONS:

11. P.G. v. Baton Rouge Restaurant (Groupe Restaurant Imvescor Restaurant Group
Inc.), 2016 HRTO 500

Holding and Analysis
The Applicant had PTSD, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and germaphobia. Previous
management had accommodated specific seating, cleaning, and service requests
related to his conditions. New management refused the accommodations, called him
“high maintenance,” ordered him to leave, and the manager stated words to the
effect of “Now I know why the police shoot crazy people like you.” The Tribunal
found discrimination in the provision of services on the ground of disability. It
rejected any justification based on policy or convenience and awarded $12,000 for
injury to dignity, feelings and self-respect. This remains one of the clearest
publicly reported examples of a successful claim arising from the denial or
effective ejection from a commercial service because of behaviour or presentation
tied to mental illness.

Application to the Present Claim
This decision confirms that mental disabilities are fully protected under the Code
and that service providers cannot refuse or withdraw accommodations, or treat a
person adversely, simply because of disability-related presentation. Offensive or
stereotypical language is treated as further evidence of discriminatory attitude.
In the Applicant’s case, any differential treatment by the Toronto Police Services
Hate Crimes Unit or related personnel that is linked to mental-health presentation,
disability-related behaviour, or the need for accommodation is governed by the same
principles. The $12,000 award also provides a useful benchmark for
injury-to-dignity damages where a person with mental disability is publicly
humiliated or subjected to adverse treatment.

1. Andrews v. Law Society of British Columbia, [1989] 1 S.C.R. 143

Holding and Analysis
This is the foundational Supreme Court of Canada decision on section 15 of the
Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms. The Court rejected formal equality
(identical treatment) in favour of substantive equality. It held that a citizenship
requirement for admission to the bar created a distinction based on an analogous
ground and was discriminatory because it imposed a burden on permanent residents
without regard to individual merit. McIntyre J. stated that the purpose of section
15 is to ensure that every individual is recognized as equally deserving of
concern, respect, and consideration. Discrimination exists where a distinction
based on an enumerated or analogous ground has the effect of imposing a
disadvantage or withholding a benefit.

Application to the Present Claim
The substantive-equality principle established in Andrews requires the Tribunal to
look at the actual impact of the Respondent’s actions on the Applicant as a person
with a mental disability. Any differential treatment, failure to inquire into or
accommodate disability-related needs, or adverse handling of the Applicant’s
interactions with the Hate Crimes Unit must be assessed for its real-world effect
on a vulnerable person, not merely for formal neutrality. Victories under the
Andrews framework succeed when the claimant demonstrates precisely this kind of
differential impact. The Applicant has done so.

17. Peel Law Association v. Pieters, 2013 ONCA 396

Holding and Analysis
The Ontario Court of Appeal upheld a human-rights finding of racial discrimination.
The Court confirmed that the protected characteristic need only be a factor in the
adverse treatment; a strict causal nexus focused on conscious intent is not
required. Circumstantial evidence and reasonable inferences drawn from the totality
of the circumstances are sufficient to establish a prima facie case.

Application to the Present Claim
The same reasoning applies fully to disability. The Applicant need not prove that
mental disability was the sole or conscious motive for any differential treatment
or failure to accommodate by the Hate Crimes Unit. It is enough that disability was
a factor. Circumstantial evidence, the overall context of the interactions, and the
Applicant’s known mental-health history and extreme vulnerability may support the
necessary inference. The decision provides strong appellate authority for a
flexible, effects-based approach to proving the connection between the protected
ground and the adverse treatment.

5. Withler v. Canada (Attorney General), 2011 SCC 12

Holding and Analysis
The Court rejected the requirement of a rigid “mirror comparator group” and
emphasized a flexible, contextual analysis of whether a distinction perpetuates
disadvantage.

Application to the Present Claim
The relevant comparison is between persons without mental disability (who receive
ordinary treatment and accommodation from police services) and persons with mental
disability (who experience differential treatment or failure to accommodate).
Withler confirms that the Tribunal need not insist on a perfect mirror; it is
sufficient that the conduct creates a distinction on the ground of disability that
reinforces disadvantage. The Applicant’s documented history and the circumstances
of his interactions with the Hate Crimes Unit supply the concrete differential
impact.
