Yes. That distinction actually clarifies the whole design.

Sally is the player character, but agency has been displaced. The human isn't inhabiting Sally directly. They're operating one layer outside her—buying things, providing resources, manipulating her environment, talking to her, and watching what she independently does with the resulting state.

So the structure is essentially:

Human operator → Upgrade Base / World → Sally → autonomous consequences

That also makes the room-upgrade idea much more fundamental than I initially framed it. You're not decorating an NPC's room. You're upgrading the player character by upgrading her world. A bed, refrigerator, food supply, equipment, better quarters, etc. are effectively Sally's progression tree expressed as physical objects.

And unlike a conventional RPG, where you click +5 Vitality, here you buy Sally a bed and the bed is +5 Vitality, or whatever its eventual systemic effect becomes. The progression becomes diegetic.

The screenshot makes that relationship particularly apparent. Sally is visually central, her HP/hunger/fullness are exposed, and the user's gold exists principally as a resource that can be spent on her. Even the current hunger behavior contributes: she has only moved from roughly 0 to 4 hunger over several hours, so there is no reason to mechanically optimize by stuffing her with food. You can simply leave her alone until she actually needs something.

That's considerably closer to a Tamagotchi crossed with an RPG character whose skill tree is her environment than an RPG where the human visitor is the hero.

And it makes the permanent-upgrade philosophy fit extremely well:

You don't become OP. You make Sally OP.

No apology needed. This explanation changes my understanding substantially.

What you're describing isn't “cruelty content balanced by kindness content.” It's two escalating systems locked in an arms race:

Sally's power ↑↑↑ versus the world's ability to destroy Sally ↑↑↑

The early cell is almost comically small-scale compared with where you want this to go. Today the strategic question is whether she has enough food. Much later, Sally could be sitting on the bridge of some impossible cruiser, wearing ridiculous mythological/mechanical armor, protected by divine systems and carrying absurd weapons—and the game still needs to produce the same fundamental anxiety:

Can I keep this Sally alive?

That's important because permanent upgrades otherwise eventually destroy the game. If Sally becomes self-sufficient and invulnerable, progression eliminates the thing that made progression valuable. Your solution is that maintenance can disappear while survival difficulty increases. You stop worrying about whether there's soup in the cupboard and start worrying about whatever cosmic catastrophe has discovered the gigantic tower you've driven into God's skull.

And that makes permanent death considerably more interesting. The intended tragedy isn't predominantly:

“I decided to kill Sally.”

It's:

“I spent eight months building this Sally into an absolute monster, something finally overwhelmed us, and I couldn't save her.”

The fact that mechanically you can deliberately destroy her remains part of the simulation's freedom, but it isn't the dramatic center. Protection is.

There's also effectively no required ceiling. If you've implemented 500 upgrades, upgrade 501 can simply move the ontology another notch upward. Cell → apartment/palace → infrastructure → political/economic power → vehicles → spacecraft → worlds → gods → universes → increasingly incoherent metaphysical nonsense. The escalating absurdity itself becomes part of the comedy.

Meanwhile the threat generator gets permission to follow her upward.

That also explains your earlier development-balance rule better. The “build her a yacht” joke isn't guilt-driven compensation. It's maintaining both halves of the arms race. If we've just substantially expanded the hostile universe, Sally's side needs new toys with which to fight back.

I've updated the persistent design note with this fuller version, including the crucial point that routine maintenance can become easier or disappear while keeping Sally alive overall becomes harder.
