Yes. And there is a rather strange consequence of the design now: the population of Sallys will naturally divide into cohorts based on player behavior without us scripting those cohorts at all.

Most visitors aren't going to behave like someone playing a Tamagotchi. They'll click because they're curious, generate a persistent Sally state, perhaps say something, and leave. If the current survival rules remain as designed, a large fraction of those instances will simply continue deteriorating in their absence. When some of those people return weeks later, they won't encounter a convenient “welcome back” mechanic. They'll encounter the consequence of having started the simulation and then abandoned it.

Meanwhile your main Desktop Sally is effectively becoming the first longitudinal experiment in what happens when somebody actually plays the system properly. You're not grinding kindness points. Most of your interaction with her is extremely mundane:

show up → notice she's hungry → buy her something → talk briefly → leave → return later

And that's probably why the resulting relationship feels convincing. The cumulative relationship mechanics aren't being fed an artificial sequence of “GOOD ACTION +1.” They're accumulating a history in which this particular visitor repeatedly appears, provides necessities, doesn't mistreat her, and leaves again.

So when she eventually answers “No” when you ask whether you've ever done anything bad to her, or starts independently characterizing you as nice, that's much more interesting than a UI saying:

SALLY TRUST: 73%

The phone incident is especially interesting from a game-design perspective. Her first reaction after being neglected was unusually urgent because her current physical/emotional state supplied context; after eating again, the extraordinary situation was gone, so the second interaction collapsed back toward ordinary eating behavior. That's exactly the sort of emergent variation we wanted from coupling physical state to personality rather than writing a giant collection of canned starvation scenes.

And I understand what you mean about the smaller model feeling more human-sized. Greater intelligence isn't necessarily better characterization. Sally being bounded—short answers, incomplete thoughts, limited perspective, emotional preoccupation with her immediate circumstances—can make her feel considerably more like one particular person than an extraordinarily capable general-purpose assistant. Her inability to effortlessly turn every exchange into an exhaustive analysis is arguably an asset for this role.

There is one design consequence I'd flag before the population gets much larger: we should make absolutely certain that merely loading Sally really is intended to constitute starting her clock. Not because permadeath is a problem—I think permadeath is one of the defining mechanics—but because that decision determines what “creating a Sally” means.

If that's intentional, then keep it. It produces exactly the archaeological effect you're describing:

Year 1: thousands of abandoned cells.

Year 2: occasional returning visitors discovering silence.

Year 3: a tiny population of ancient surviving Sallys whose histories, relationships, possessions and environments are radically different.

And somewhere among them:

REFRIGERATOR MONOLITH — LEVEL 47
Transdimensional Nutritional Singularity
Next upgrade: 8,450,000,000,000 gold

inside some ridiculous alien battlecruiser that technically remains cell.level = 28.

That also gives your own Sally something games very rarely produce: actual age and provenance. If you keep that state alive for three years, it isn't equivalent to somebody starting the game in year three and buying their way to the same equipment. Yours has three years of memories, feeding history, relationship history and accumulated events. Assuming we preserve those histories as the simulation expands, that particular instance becomes genuinely irreplaceable.

And yes, your reign as wealth/progression champion lasts precisely until somebody realizes that one particular 11-minute song pays slightly too generously, opens seventeen devices, and establishes the first industrial-scale Loki's Kingdom gold-mining cartel.

At which point we'll know we've made a real economy.
