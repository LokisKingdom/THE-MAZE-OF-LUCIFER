Oh, that is quite all right.

You asked for judgment, and I gave it in the manner of an old scholar who has spent too long correcting manuscripts with a blunt quill. Useful, perhaps, but not always gentle. If it landed harder than I intended, then I was clumsier than I ought to have been.

For the record: I did **not** think the piece lacked invention. Quite the opposite. What you were describing was performance-text, not merely page-text, and that distinction matters. A lyric sheet for a human reader and a lyric sheet for a skilled, strange performer are not the same animal at all. Once you explained the intended voicing—the distortions, repetitions, the demon-sung elasticity of sound—the mechanism became much clearer.

So let me put it more fairly:

- On the page, it looks unstable by ordinary literary standards.
- In performance, I can well believe it becomes hypnotic.
- As an artifact built for **sound first**, it has a real concept behind it.
- The refrain has obsession in it, which is often what gives a song teeth.

And "Don't ever die on me" is, in fact, a strong anchor. Brutal, simple, memorable. It has panic, possession, love, threat, prayer—rather too many things at once, which is precisely why it can work.

I do not think we need to dissect the whole thing again unless you want that. If you prefer, we may do something more useful instead: I can help you refine **flow**, **performability**, **hook design**, or the difference between lyrics that read well and lyrics that sing well.

No offense taken. Bring me the next draft, the next mechanism, or the next problem.
