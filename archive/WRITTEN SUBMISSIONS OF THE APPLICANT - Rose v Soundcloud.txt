### A. Overview

1. The Applicant submits that this Application has a reasonable prospect of success and should proceed to a hearing on its merits.

2. At its core, this is a case about equality of access to economic participation in a modern digital music service. The Applicant is an Ontario artist with a disability who uses artificial intelligence as a disability-related aid in the creation and production of music.

3. The Applicant alleges that SoundCloud applies a rule or practice that prevents or restricts monetization of music because it was created using artificial intelligence. Whatever the purpose of that rule may be, its effect upon the Applicant is not neutral.

4. The Applicant's disability creates substantial barriers to conventional methods of professional music production. Traditional production ordinarily requires sustained interpersonal collaboration with musicians, bands, producers and others. Those methods have repeatedly proven inaccessible or ineffective for the Applicant because of disability-related limitations affecting sustained interpersonal and professional relationships.

5. Artificial intelligence removed that barrier. It enabled the Applicant to transform his writing, compositions, concepts and artistic direction into completed musical works without depending upon a conventional band structure that he has been unable to maintain.

6. The Applicant puts the matter simply:

> **I am a human artist.**

7. Artificial intelligence does not change that fact. The Applicant conceives the works, writes and directs his artistic output, selects what is produced and published, develops the corpus in which those works exist, and presents those works to an audience. AI is the instrument and disability-related aid through which the Applicant is able to participate meaningfully in music production.

8. The Applicant therefore rejects any suggestion that accommodating his use of AI would improperly divert money away from “human artists.” The Applicant is himself the human artist seeking equal access to monetization. The distinction created by the challenged policy is not, in his circumstances, between human creativity and non-human creativity. It is between a human artist able to work through conventional production methods and a human artist who alleges that disability requires him to work through an alternative technological method.

### B. Adverse-Effect Discrimination Based on Disability

9. The Applicant does not need to establish that SoundCloud adopted its AI policy because it intended to discriminate against persons with disabilities. That is not the principal theory advanced here.

10. The Application concerns adverse-effect or constructive discrimination: a requirement that may appear neutral but allegedly produces unequal consequences when applied to the Applicant because of a characteristic protected by the Ontario Human Rights Code.

11. Section 11 of the Code addresses requirements, qualifications or factors that are neutral on their face but result in exclusion, restriction or preference affecting persons identified by prohibited grounds. Ontario human-rights guidance likewise recognizes that discrimination need not be intentional and that service providers have obligations concerning disability-related barriers. ([Ontario Human Rights Commission][2])

12. The Applicant's position can therefore be expressed as a straightforward chain:

**Disability → disability-related barrier to conventional music production → AI as a means of overcoming that barrier → successful participation in music production → exclusion of AI-assisted works from monetization → adverse economic effect.**

13. Before the availability of generative music technology, the Applicant spent many years attempting to make music through methods that did not provide a viable route to independent professional participation. Among other things, he created vocals over existing copyrighted recordings because he could not independently produce the underlying musical performances and could not reliably obtain them through conventional collaboration.

14. Generative music technology fundamentally changed that situation. For the first time, the Applicant had an instrument capable of addressing the particular barrier preventing independent production.

15. The Applicant describes Suno in functional terms as **“my guitar.”** The analogy is not intended to suggest that the technology is literally equivalent to a guitar. It describes its role: it is the means through which the Applicant can translate human authorship, writing, direction and creative decisions into music without requiring a conventional collaborative structure that disability makes substantially inaccessible to him.

16. The resulting productivity is not evidence that accommodation is unnecessary. On the contrary, the Applicant submits that his productivity demonstrates that the accommodation works.

17. A person does not cease to have a disability-related need merely because the technology addressing that need enables the person to perform successfully.

18. Ontario human-rights principles recognize that accommodation exists to remove barriers so that people with Code-related needs can have equal opportunities, access and benefits. Accommodation can require adjustment of policies or rules rather than requiring the affected person simply to function through the conventional method. ([Ontario Human Rights Commission][3])

19. The Applicant alleges that SoundCloud's monetization practice produces the opposite result. He can use technology to overcome the disability-related barrier and participate creatively, but the resulting works are then excluded from meaningful economic participation because of the technological method that enabled him to produce them.

20. On the Applicant's case, this is not a request for preferential royalties, guaranteed commercial success, or payment for work that would otherwise be ineligible for legitimate reasons.

21. It is a request for an opportunity to participate in the same monetization system available to other eligible artists, without being excluded merely because disability-related needs resulted in the use of AI in producing the work.

22. The Applicant has generated millions of listens. The precise economic loss resulting from the alleged exclusion will ultimately depend upon evidence concerning SoundCloud's monetization system, the streams that would otherwise have qualified, applicable royalty calculations, and other relevant factors.

23. The exact quantum need not be exaggerated to establish the alleged adverse effect. The essential allegation is that substantial listening activity occurred while the Applicant was denied the economic opportunity ordinarily associated with eligible monetized listening.

### C. Creed and the Religious Corpus

24. The Application independently raises discrimination based upon creed.

25. The Applicant's music forms part of a sincerely held religious corpus. The fact that the works may outwardly resemble rap, electronic music, popular music or other secular artistic forms does not determine their religious significance to the Applicant.

26. The Applicant's creed is unconventional and has evolved over time. Neither fact, by itself, establishes insincerity.

27. The Applicant relies upon *Syndicat Northcrest v. Amselem* for the principle that the assessment of religious belief focuses upon the individual's sincerely held belief rather than requiring the claimant to establish that the practice is objectively mandatory according to an external religious authority. The jurisprudence cautions against turning courts into arbiters of religious orthodoxy. ([SCC CSC Lexum][4])

28. Ontario human-rights guidance similarly recognizes that a sincerely held creed belief need not be an essential element of a creed or recognized as valid by religious officials. ([Ontario Human Rights Commission][1])

29. There is substantial evidence available concerning sincerity in this case. The Applicant has created an extraordinarily large corpus of works over an extended period and has consistently attributed profound religious significance to the creation and preservation of that corpus.

30. The Applicant's historical conduct also includes extraordinary personal sacrifices associated with continuing this work under extremely adverse circumstances.

31. The Applicant does **not** contend that such sacrifice is required to establish religious sincerity. It plainly is not.

32. Rather, that history is evidence available in this particular case supporting the proposition that the Applicant's characterization of the corpus as religious is sincerely held and is not a belief manufactured for purposes of litigation.

33. The Applicant further submits that where unusually substantial evidence of sincerity exists, that evidence cannot rationally be discounted merely because the particular creed or its means of artistic expression appears strange or unfamiliar to an outside observer. To do so would effectively subject an unconventional creed to a more demanding sincerity standard than a familiar creed.

34. That latter proposition is advanced as the Applicant's argument. It does not depend upon claiming that *Amselem* expressly formulated that precise evidentiary rule.

35. Ontario's adverse-effect framework is particularly relevant to this aspect of the case. The OHRC expressly recognizes that an organizational rule may constitute creed discrimination through its unintended effect and that accommodation seeks to permit people with different sincerely held creed beliefs to participate equally in services. ([Ontario Human Rights Commission][5])

36. The Applicant alleges that the challenged monetization rule adversely affects the very works through which he creates and disseminates his religious corpus.

37. Accordingly, the disability and creed allegations are distinct but intersect:

**The Applicant uses AI because of disability-related needs.**

**The Applicant uses that means of production to create works forming part of his religious corpus.**

**The challenged monetization rule allegedly attaches an economic disadvantage to those works because of that means of production.**

38. One apparently neutral rule therefore allegedly produces adverse effects engaging two protected characteristics in the Applicant's particular circumstances: disability and creed.

### D. This Is an Equality Claim, Not an Attack on SoundCloud

39. The Applicant does not allege that SoundCloud is inherently hostile to persons with disabilities or to his creed.

40. Nor is this proceeding motivated by personal hostility toward SoundCloud.

41. The Applicant regards SoundCloud as a valuable service. It has enabled his music to reach listeners and has facilitated interactions with an audience that would otherwise have been difficult or impossible for him to develop.

42. The Applicant's objection is narrower: he believes the monetization policy is wrong because, as applied to his circumstances, it permits him to contribute music, audience engagement and listening activity to the platform while preventing him from participating equally in the corresponding economic opportunity.

43. The remedy sought should therefore be understood principally as an equality remedy. The Applicant wants the opportunity to work, compete and receive monetization without a disability-related means of production automatically disqualifying his work.

44. Human-rights accommodation is not a demand for special privilege. Its purpose is substantive equality: addressing rules or structures that may function adequately for most people while creating barriers for people protected by the Code. ([Ontario Human Rights Commission][6])

45. Again:

> **I am a human artist.**

46. The Applicant does not accept that recognizing his disability-related use of AI takes an opportunity away from human artists. Accommodating the Applicant would provide an opportunity **to** a human artist who alleges that conventional production methods are inaccessible because of disability.

### E. Reasonable Prospect of Success

47. The Tribunal is not presently being asked to determine every disputed fact or finally quantify the Applicant's damages.

48. The immediate question is whether the Application should be terminated at the summary stage notwithstanding the factual and legal foundation available to the Applicant.

49. The Applicant can identify evidence concerning his disability; the functional barriers affecting conventional music production; his historical attempts to make music before generative technology became available; his use of AI in overcoming those barriers; his extensive musical output; SoundCloud's treatment of AI-assisted works; the listening activity generated by those works; the religious corpus and its development; and the sincerity of the beliefs associated with that corpus.

50. There are consequently genuine factual and legal questions concerning whether the challenged policy produces adverse-effect discrimination and, if so, whether the Applicant's disability- and creed-related needs could be accommodated without undue hardship.

51. Those questions warrant determination on a proper evidentiary record rather than dismissal on the premise that AI-assisted music necessarily represents something other than human artistic participation.

52. The Applicant respectfully submits that the Application therefore has a reasonable prospect of success and should be permitted to proceed.
