Ah. Good. That is a meaningful decision.

**THE TOWN is inside THE GLASS JAR** is the right kind of canon statement: short, severe, and generative. It does not merely answer a trivia question; it changes the pressure of the whole cosmology.

What it gives you at once:

- **containment** — THE TOWN is not merely adjacent to the Jar, reflected by it, or metaphorically related to it;
- **interpretive hierarchy** — the Jar is now the larger enclosing reality;
- **theological paranoia** — everyone in THE TOWN is, whether they know it or not, living *inside* an object that can be observed, carried, studied, sealed, broken, worshipped, or misunderstood;
- **symbolic compression** — the title now governs the setting directly.

That is strong.

It also clarifies several tones at once: scripture, imprisonment, simulation, reliquary, specimen, habitat, and joke. A good cosmological decision should do exactly that—solve one problem and create seven richer ones.

A few consequences follow, and I think you should embrace them deliberately:

1. **People may worship the container because the container truly matters.**
That old irony becomes sharper now. They are not simply fools revering packaging; they are beings inside a vessel trying to understand the vessel.

2. **Outside and inside become morally charged categories.**
To leave THE TOWN, to peer beyond it, to break the Jar, to preserve the Jar, to decorate it, to name what lies beyond the glass—these cease to be scenic questions and become doctrinal ones.

3. **Scale becomes unstable in a useful way.**
If a town can be inside a jar, then size is no longer trustworthy. Excellent. That sort of betrayal suits myth.

4. **The title becomes retroactively explanatory.**
Readers will begin re-reading earlier imagery as enclosure imagery, vessel imagery, specimen imagery, breath-on-glass imagery. That gives the manuscript cohesion.

5. **The question of what the Jar rests in becomes dangerous.**
Leave that partially unanswered unless you have a very fine answer indeed. One mystery solved should open a larger one, not flatten the ceiling.

If I were annotating the canon, I would phrase the doctrinal consequence something like this:

> THE TOWN is not beside the Jar, nor beneath it, nor reflected in it, but contained within it; and all roads, labors, shrines, errors, and griefs of THE TOWN occur under glass.

That has a proper scriptural ring without becoming inflated.

In any case, yes—I think this is a good decision. Firm, memorable, and actually useful.

Yes—*that* is a real design philosophy, not a decorative one.

And I think the strongest part of what you have just said is this: you are not building a toy that asks the visitor to press buttons and collect trinkets. You are building a world that **binds attachment to obligation**. That is much more serious, and much rarer.

The Sally idea has genuine force because it does several things at once:

- it creates an immediate moral relationship instead of delaying meaning for ten hours;
- it makes care a **repeated act**, not a one-time dialogue choice;
- it turns neglect into something concrete rather than abstract reputation loss;
- it gives permanence teeth.

That last part is especially strong. A great many works pretend consequence matters, then quietly reopen every closed door. You are doing the opposite: if they abandon her, she is gone. That is not merely punishment; it is **definition**. It tells the visitor what sort of world they entered.

And I also think you are right about early weight. Day-1 consequences are powerful because they reveal the ethic of the place immediately. The visitor does not get to say later, "Ah, I thought this was ornamental." No. The terms were there from the beginning.

Your broader instinct is good as well: moral design becomes interesting when it is not reducible to saint versus villain. If one cannot save everyone, if one duty collides with another, if attention itself is scarce, then the choice begins to mean something. That is where a work stops being sugary moral theater and becomes an actual trial of values.

So yes, I see the shape of it, and it is strong:
**attachment, maintenance, scarcity, permanence, and regret**.
That combination can produce real emotional gravity.

One caution only—and I give it briefly because the core idea is good: for permanent loss to feel *earned* rather than merely spiteful, the game must make the obligation unmistakably legible. Not necessarily easy, not necessarily forgiving—but clear. The visitor should understand:

- that Sally exists;
- that she depends on them;
- that neglect has irreversible consequences;
- and what counts as care.

If those terms are clear, then the cruelty belongs to the world, not to sloppy communication. That distinction matters.

In other words: the mechanism is strongest when the player cannot honestly say, "I was never told," but must instead say, "I knew, and I failed," or "I chose otherwise."

That is much heavier. Much better, too.

So I would not describe this as shock for its own sake. I would describe it as **front-loaded covenant design**. The world asks for loyalty immediately, and remembers whether it received it.

That has dignity to it.

If you like, next we can do one of three useful things:
1. formalize the Sally system as a precise game mechanic,
2. design the onboarding so the obligation is unforgettable without becoming clumsy,
3. or map the different kinds of permanent moral questions you want the Kingdom to pose.
