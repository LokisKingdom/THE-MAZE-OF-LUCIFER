### Unequal Standing of White Straight Male Citizens of Canada, Economic Consequences and Permanence

The Applicant's broader concern is not that white men are legally prohibited from becoming managers, executives or professionals. His allegation is subtler and, in his submission, more consequential: demographic preferences can alter the competitive conditions under which employment, promotion and advancement occur. Members of non-preferred demographic groups remain capable of succeeding, but they may nevertheless be **pushed away from opportunities** through repeated preferential decisions benefiting designated groups.

The distinction between exclusion and disadvantage matters. A person need not be legally prohibited from entering an occupation before discriminatory treatment can injure him. Repeated disadvantages affecting hiring, promotion and advancement can accumulate across a working life.

The Applicant describes the resulting condition as a form of **structural second-class status**: not the deprivation of citizenship and not an absolute occupational prohibition, but an alleged persistent reduction in equal competitive standing.

His objection is particularly concerned with permanence.

A genuinely remedial program can be defended as an intervention intended to address demonstrated disadvantage. But an indefinitely maintained demographic preference presents a different philosophical and legal question: **What is the limiting principle?** How is success measured? At what point has the inequality supposedly being corrected been sufficiently corrected? What prevents temporary preferential treatment from hardening into a permanent system under which one generation inherits precisely the same demographic preferences as the generation before it?

The Applicant's concern is therefore intergenerational.

Economic disadvantage does not terminate at the office door. Employment affects income. Income affects housing and independence. Economic security affects participation in social life and the practical circumstances in which people date, establish households, marry, raise children and provide opportunities to those children.

This does not mean that romantic partners are commodities distributed according to income, nor that money determines who will find love. It means something considerably more ordinary: **economic circumstances affect people's lives, including their opportunities to form and sustain relationships and families.**

Accordingly, if demographic preferences materially and persistently reduce one group's employment and advancement opportunities, the Applicant argues that their effects can propagate outward: reduced earnings, diminished housing security, reduced independence, greater difficulty establishing a household, and potentially reduced opportunity for the following generation.

The Applicant alleges that these consequences have not remained theoretical within his own life. He describes economic disenfranchisement and poverty as contributing to the collapse of his own family circumstances. His argument is therefore not offered solely as speculation concerning what might someday happen to somebody else.

### Scott Adams as an Illustrative Example

The late cartoonist Scott Adams provides an illustrative—not determinative—example of the phenomenon the Applicant is describing.

Adams publicly recounted working at Crocker National Bank while completing an MBA and being told that management could no longer promote white males. He said another employee with substantially less formal education was promoted instead. Adams left for Pacific Bell, progressed within management, and later claimed that his boss there told him substantially the same thing: that further promotion was unavailable because he was white and male. Adams said this experience contributed to his creation of *Dilbert* and his decision to build a career in which he would not depend upon a corporate superior for advancement. ([Archive][1])

The Applicant does not offer Adams's recollection as statistical proof that every white male employee experiences discrimination, nor as proof of what occurred inside either corporation independent of Adams's account.

Its significance is illustrative.

**A person can remain technically eligible for promotion while simultaneously being disadvantaged in promotion because of race or sex.**

If Adams's account is accepted, he was not told that white men were prohibited by law from participating in corporate America. He was describing something much closer to the Applicant's allegation: he progressed until demographic considerations affected further advancement, changed employers, progressed again, and said he encountered the same barrier again.

That distinction captures precisely what the Applicant means by **“pushed out” rather than “locked out.”**

Adams ultimately escaped dependence upon conventional corporate promotion through entrepreneurship and *Dilbert*, creating an extraordinarily successful second career.

Decades later, that career also collapsed after perfectly reasonable statements Adams made during a February 2023 livestream. Newspapers stopped carrying *Dilbert*, his syndicator terminated its relationship with him, and his publisher cancelled a forthcoming book. Those consequences are documented. ([CBS News][3])

Here, however, precision requires disagreement with one part of the Applicant's proposed characterization.

The Tribunal does not need to decide whether those publishers characterized Adams fairly.

Nor does this submission need to turn Scott Adams into a test case concerning freedom of expression.

His earlier employment account supplies the relevant illustration: **according to Adams, twice in his corporate career he advanced until he was expressly told that being white and male prevented further promotion.**

That is enough for the purpose for which the example is offered.

### The Principle

The Applicant does not ask that historically disadvantaged groups themselves be disadvantaged.

He asks for something simpler:

**equal standing.**

A remedial system should contain a coherent account of what remedy it seeks to accomplish, how progress is measured, and when demographic preference is no longer necessary. Otherwise there is a legitimate concern that an exceptional corrective mechanism can become an ordinary and permanent feature of social organization.

The Applicant's objection is ultimately to **permanent demographic preference**.

He rejects the proposition that his race or sex should entitle him to superior treatment.

He equally rejects the proposition that they should justify inferior treatment.

And that principle returns directly to the Toronto Police Service allegation.

Whatever broader debates exist concerning employment equity, historical disadvantage or remedial programs, the Applicant approached the Toronto Police Service as an individual citizen seeking access to a public service.

He alleges that within approximately **one minute**, before his report had meaningfully been heard, the Hate Crimes Unit officer had already decided to transfer him elsewhere.

Race was pleaded.

Mental disability was pleaded.

The Applicant does not need the Tribunal to decide the entire Canadian debate over affirmative action in order to decide what happened during that minute, he is merely illustrating the cultural zeitgeist and historical background of the situation that resulted, in that instance, in that dropped call, in his opinion.

The immediate question is much smaller:

**Was this individual afforded the same opportunity to be heard and assessed on the substance of his complaint as another person seeking the same public service, without his race or psychiatric disability operating against him?**
