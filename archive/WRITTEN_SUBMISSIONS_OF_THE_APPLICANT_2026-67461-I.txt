1. SECTION 1: THE CASE AGAINST CAMH

Historical and Systemic Context Poverty, Deprivation, Institutionalization and Psychiatric Stability
1. Purpose and Scope of This Historical Section This section is provided primarily as historical and systemic context. It is not
intended to transform every historical experience described below into a separate allegation requiring adjudication, nor is
every criticism of Ontario's psychiatric system advanced as an independent claim for relief.
The context is nevertheless material to the Tribunal's understanding of the events in issue.
The events at issue did not arise in a vacuum. They occurred within a psychiatric system that possesses extraordinary power
over persons diagnosed with serious mental illness: the power to assess them, characterize their behaviour, record
conclusions about their mental state, substantially influence how police and other institutions respond to them, and, under
circumstances prescribed by law, participate in processes resulting in involuntary confinement and treatment.
My criticism of that system therefore forms part of the background necessary to understand my relationship with CAMH, my
concern about the relationship between CAMH and the Toronto Police Service, and the significance I attach to apparently
ordinary disputes about credibility, independence, work, behaviour and psychiatric stability.
The objective is twofold, and the objectives are parallel rather than contradictory.
I seek appropriate relief concerning the matters properly before the Tribunal.
I also intend to describe, within the rules and proper scope of these proceedings, the institutional system in which those
events occurred.
The latter should not be discarded merely because some experiences that explain the system predate the period directly at
issue. Historical evidence can remain important to understanding a contemporary institutional relationship without every
historical event itself becoming a separate claim. Where a historical event materially explains my fear, credibility

concerns, reaction to police or psychiatric intervention, or the consequences I say flowed from those
institutions, I ask that it be retained as context even if it is not itself an independently adjudicable claim.
2. The Cycle of Disability, Poverty and Psychiatric Crisis A central criticism concerns the relationship between disability
income, poverty, food security, housing security and psychiatric stability.
Ontario Disability Support Program recipients may already begin from a position of substantial vulnerability. A person who
cannot reliably afford both adequate housing and adequate food is not merely experiencing an accounting problem. Those
material conditions affect the circumstances under which that person is expected to remain psychiatrically stable.
This criticism is directed expressly at the policy choices of Premier Doug Ford's Ontario government insofar as those choices
determine or perpetuate inadequate disability assistance.
The argument requires precise delineation.
I am not arguing that starvation creates schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, or another underlying psychiatric disease.
I have a diagnosed psychiatric illness. The argument here does not deny the existence of serious mental illness and does not
propose hunger as its original cause.
The proposition is narrower and more precise:
Food is a prerequisite of human functioning. Deprive a human being of adequate food for sufficiently long and that human
being will deteriorate.
The precise breaking point varies from person to person. The underlying physiological principle does not.
Hunger causes suffering. Prolonged and severe deprivation can produce weakness, irritability, desperation, impaired
concentration, emotional dysregulation and increasingly abnormal behaviour. A human being possesses no infinite reserve
against starvation.
This principle applies to people with psychiatric illnesses and people without them.
There is therefore no contradiction between saying that a person has an underlying psychiatric disorder and saying that
hunger, homelessness, exhaustion, fear, isolation or other severe environmental stressors can worsen that person's
condition. The original diagnosis and the subsequent deterioration are different questions.
A person with serious mental illness who has been forcibly confined, then discharged does not cease being biologically
human upon leaving the hospital. That person requires food, shelter, sleep, safety and the ordinary material foundations of
life. If those foundations are removed, deterioration is not mysterious.
It is a foreseeable consequence.
3. Hunger, Suffering and the Human Breaking Point The point about food must not be misunderstood as a theory concerning
the origin of psychiatric disease.
Take psychiatric diagnosis out of the equation entirely.
Take an otherwise psychologically healthy human being and subject that person to sufficiently severe and prolonged
deprivation.
Eventually the person suffers.
Eventually the person reaches a breaking point.

A starving person may cry.
A suffering person may scream.
A desperate person who repeatedly asks for food and receives none may begin shouting for somebody to respond.
Someone who was calm at the beginning of the ordeal may no longer be calm after prolonged deprivation.
The precise temporal threshold is not determinative. Different human beings have different physical reserves and different
tolerances for suffering. What matters is the elementary proposition that human beings deteriorate when deprived of the
necessities required to sustain them.
That proposition becomes especially important when the resulting behaviour occurs inside a psychiatric institution.
4. When Ordinary Human Distress Becomes Psychiatric Evidence A psychiatric environment creates an unusual interpretive
problem.
Behaviour occurring inside such an institution is observed through a psychiatric lens. A patient may already possess a
serious diagnosis. Staff are charged with observing behaviour. Observations enter clinical records. Those records may
subsequently influence decisions concerning treatment, liberty and future assessments.
But a psychiatric patient remains capable of ordinary human reactions.
A person can simultaneously have a psychiatric disorder and be legitimately angry.
A person can simultaneously have a psychiatric disorder and be frightened by confinement.
A person can simultaneously have a psychiatric disorder and become irritable because he is hungry.
A person can simultaneously have a psychiatric disorder and react strongly to assault, humiliation, sleep deprivation, isolation
or prolonged uncertainty.
Consequently, an institution must remain capable of distinguishing symptoms attributable to psychiatric pathology from
behaviour produced, aggravated or materially influenced by circumstances surrounding the patient.
Otherwise a dangerous circularity becomes possible:
institutional condition → distress → distressed behaviour → documentation of distressed behaviour → distressed
behaviour interpreted as further evidence of psychiatric instability.
The institution may then treat as evidence of the patient's disorder behaviour that the institutional environment itself helped
create.
That distinction is substantive, not merely semantic.
Where an institution exercises substantial authority over a patient's liberty, that distinction can become profoundly
consequential.
5. My Experience of Confinement and Escalating Distress My historical experiences of psychiatric confinement illustrate this
problem.
I have experienced situations in which I believed I had legitimate reasons to object to what was happening to me. Yet once a
person is understood primarily as a psychiatric patient, forceful objection can itself become susceptible to psychiatric
interpretation.
The underlying message—
I am angry because I believe what is happening to me is wrong—
can disappear.
What remains in the institutional description may instead become:
the patient is angry, agitated, yelling or behaving irrationally.
My experience of isolation made this problem particularly stark.
I describe being placed alone in a cell-like isolation environment. Initially, I could wait calmly.
Time passed.
I became hungry.
I requested food.
As the isolation and hunger continued, my condition deteriorated. Eventually, obtaining attention required increasingly forceful
communication.
The institutional observation emerging at the end of such a sequence can be technically accurate and profoundly incomplete:
the patient was yelling and screaming.
But that description, standing alone, destroys the chronology that gives the behaviour meaning.
It removes:
The patient waited.
The patient became hungry.
The patient requested food.

The request was not satisfied.
The deprivation continued.
The patient became increasingly distressed.
The patient eventually yelled.
One account merely describes behaviour.
The other explains behaviour.
Psychiatric assessment cannot fairly collapse the second into the first. The chronology underlying this criticism appears in my
original account of the isolation experience.
6. Patients Are Not Interchangeable
My criticism extends beyond isolation.
Sharing a psychiatric diagnosis does not make patients clinically, behaviourally or socially interchangeable.
People receiving psychiatric care can differ enormously in cognitive functioning, independence, behavioural presentation,
propensity toward violence, communication ability, treatment requirements and capacity for independent life.
Those differences matter.
During my institutionalizations I was confined alongside people whom I experienced as profoundly unstable, aggressive and
dangerous.
I was subjected to threats, harassment, humiliation, physical aggression and sustained behaviour that I experienced as
terrorizing.
Whatever treatment those individuals themselves required, I should not have been required to absorb their violence and
intimidation as a condition of receiving treatment for my own illness.
I am not similarly situated to another patient merely because both of us possess psychiatric diagnoses.
A comparatively high-functioning, independently functioning and non-violent patient should not automatically be confined in
close quarters with patients exhibiting persistent violent, threatening or severely disruptive behaviour.
My objection is not theoretical.
I was the person exposed to it.
From my perspective, forcing me into that environment was not merely treatment conducted under unpleasant
circumstances. The environment itself became an additional source of danger, fear and psychiatric destabilization imposed
upon me while I was confined and unable simply to leave.
The distinction between those patients and me therefore matters.
My diagnosis does not erase my individual functioning, behaviour, history or risk profile.
“Psychiatric patient” cannot become a sufficient classification for placing radically different people together and expecting a
comparatively non-violent patient to endure whatever follows.
Someone who has never experienced a locked psychiatric ward may possess a sanitized conception of what involuntary
confinement among severely unstable and sometimes violent strangers actually entails.
My historical experience was not sanitized.
I experienced those conditions as dangerous.
I experienced some of the people around me as dangerous.
I was terrorized.
And I reject the proposition that sharing a broad category of psychiatric illness made it appropriate to subject me to that
danger.
7. Confinement Is Itself a Serious Human Event The length of psychiatric confinement should not be trivialized merely
because the confinement occurs inside a hospital rather than a correctional institution.
Weeks constitute a substantial portion of a person's life.
During confinement, ordinary autonomy may be dramatically reduced. Movement is restricted. Privacy is diminished. Daily
routines are imposed externally. Access to personal property and ordinary activities may be restricted. Communication with
the outside world can become more difficult. Boredom can become profound.
For someone accustomed to working, creating, communicating freely and organizing his own day, that loss of autonomy is
not an abstraction.
It is lived hour after hour.
The therapeutic purpose of psychiatric hospitalization does not make those consequences unreal.
Nor does acknowledging those consequences require denying that hospitalization can sometimes be medically necessary.
Both propositions can be true:
psychiatric hospitalization may sometimes be necessary;
and

psychiatric hospitalization, particularly involuntary hospitalization, can itself be traumatic, destabilizing and profoundly
disruptive.
A serious psychiatric system should be capable of confronting both propositions simultaneously.
8. Food Inside and Outside the Institution Food appears at two different points in this systemic criticism.
Outside the institution, inadequate disability income can contribute to food insecurity and instability.
Inside the institution, control over food becomes part of an environment in which the patient has substantially reduced
autonomy.
The significance is not that every unpleasant meal constitutes mistreatment.
The significance is that someone deprived of control over where he lives, when he leaves, what he does and many aspects of
his daily routine becomes unusually dependent upon the institution to meet basic needs adequately.
Food is among the most basic of those needs.
When a patient's distress is being clinically observed, inadequate access to food cannot intelligently be detached from
subsequent behaviour.
Again, context matters.
9. The Economic Contradiction: A Prosperous Apparatus Surrounding an Impoverished Patient There is a further systemic
contradiction that must be stated more forcefully.
The criticism is not that CAMH employees should themselves be impoverished.
Doctors should be paid for their expertise.
Nurses should be paid for their work.
Support staff should receive decent wages.
The criticism is what the economic arrangement looks like from the other side of the desk.
The result is a relationship I regard as parasitic.
The patient can enter a publicly funded mental-health institution and encounter professionals who are secure, well- fed and
comparatively affluent, whose livelihoods are funded by a system ostensibly organized around the patient's welfare.
The patient himself can leave that encounter with an annual disability income insufficient to secure both adequate housing
and adequate food.
The institution is sustained.
Its employees are paid.
The professional class surrounding psychiatric disability can build careers, acquire property and live ordinary middle-class
lives.
Meanwhile, the supposed beneficiary can deteriorate into hunger, homelessness, isolation, depression, fear and renewed
psychiatric crisis.
This disparity is especially stark from the perspective of someone surviving on disability income. An income that may appear
merely ordinary when viewed from within the professional middle class can represent extraordinary financial security when
compared with the resources available to an ODSP recipient.
The patient sees the contrast every time he enters the institution.
One side of the desk is professionally employed, fed, clothed and economically secure.
The other side may be trying to determine whether this month's inadequate resources should be consumed by shelter, food,
medication-related necessities, transportation and the countless other requirements of remaining alive and functional.
This is not merely an aesthetic contrast between rich and poor.
The poverty of the patient can itself become part of the mechanism producing the next psychiatric crisis.
10. The Ford Government and the Architecture of the System Responsibility for creating this economic arrangement rests
substantially with the Government of Ontario and, during the period relevant to this criticism, the government of Premier
Doug Ford insofar as provincial policy determines the adequacy of disability assistance and the allocation of public
resources.
But identifying the architect does not make the resulting institutional relationship benign.
CAMH and its personnel remain participants in a system in which substantial public resources circulate around the treatment
and administration of psychiatric disability while dramatically fewer resources may reach the disabled person himself.
Calling that relationship “parasitic” is deliberately provocative.
It identifies the contradiction I intend to place before the Tribunal:
an elaborate and comparatively prosperous apparatus can exist because people like me require assistance while the people
requiring that assistance remain desperately poor.
The Ford government's responsibility for disability policy does not require blaming an individual CAMH nurse or receptionist
for setting ODSP rates.
But neither does the existence of a government policy maker erase the institutional reality experienced by the patient.

The patient does not experience government ministries as isolated boxes on an organizational chart.
The patient experiences a system.
He sees substantial resources available once he becomes a psychiatric problem requiring professional management.
He sees dramatically fewer resources available when he needs food, shelter and ordinary stability in order to remain well
outside the institution.
That is the contradiction.
11. The Parasitic Cycle This economic contradiction becomes still more troubling when viewed across the entire cycle.
Imagine a person with serious mental illness who becomes psychiatrically unstable while homeless or severely impoverished.
The person is forcibly confined in a prison-like psychiatric system.
The immediate crisis is treated.
Public resources become available.
Professionals are paid.
A bed exists.
A building exists.
Security exists.
Clinical infrastructure exists.
Food exists.
Medication exists.
During treatment, the patient remains forcibly confined in that prison-like system.
The patient is discharged.
Then the extraordinary concentration of institutional resources surrounding that patient recedes.
The person returns to the economic conditions available outside the institution.
If those conditions do not permit reliable access to housing, nutrition, safety and stability, deterioration may begin again.
Another crisis may follow.
Police or emergency services may become involved.
Another episode of forcible confinement may follow.
Again, substantial institutional resources become available.
Another period of prison-like confinement.
Another discharge.
And the cycle repeats:
poverty → deprivation and instability → deterioration → crisis → police/emergency intervention → forcible prison-like
confinement → expensive professional management → discharge into poverty → renewed deterioration.
The original version of this historical argument describes precisely this repeated movement between deprivation, forcible
confinement in a prison-like system and return to the conditions preceding the crisis.
This is why merely saying that Ontario “funds crisis instead of prevention” does not fully capture my criticism.
My criticism is darker.
The system can impoverish psychiatric patients outside the institution, concentrate severely destabilized people together
inside the institution, expose individual patients to the consequences of one another's deterioration, and then professionally
administer the resulting dysfunction at considerable public expense.
An entire professional apparatus can remain economically healthy while the human beings whose suffering justifies that
apparatus remain profoundly economically unhealthy.
That is what I mean by a parasitic relationship.
It does not mean every person employed by CAMH intends to exploit patients.
It describes the structure and its result.
12. Treatment Resistance and the Extraordinary Power of Psychiatry My historical experience also exposed me to the more
extreme possibilities contained within psychiatric medicine.
During one hospitalization, I encountered another patient who told me that he was undergoing a procedure involving his brain
in connection with treatment resistance.
I cannot independently establish from that encounter precisely what medical procedure he underwent, what his diagnosis
was, or the clinical reasoning supporting it.
I therefore do not present my understanding of another patient's procedure as established medical fact.
The encounter nevertheless affected me.

It confronted me with the reality that psychiatric medicine can, in exceptional circumstances and subject to legal and medical
requirements, extend far beyond ordinary conversation and medication.
Modern psychosurgical procedures exist. Their existence is not folklore.
That reality matters to the institutional context because psychiatric patients interact with a system possessing exceptionally
consequential forms of authority and treatment.
The appropriate conclusion is not that every psychiatrist threatens every patient with extreme intervention.
It is that the power imbalance inherent in serious psychiatric treatment is real, and consequently safeguards surrounding
autonomy, consent, credibility, individualized assessment and humane treatment deserve exceptional attention.
13. Why ODSP Is Psychiatric Policy Too The adequacy of ODSP cannot therefore be treated as wholly separate from
psychiatric policy.
If Ontario accepts that some citizens are unable, by reason of disability, to obtain sufficient income through ordinary
employment, the amount provided to those citizens has consequences.
If that amount is insufficient to maintain basic material stability, the resulting deprivation does not disappear merely because
governmental responsibility has been administratively divided between different ministries and institutions.
The psychiatric system encounters the person after deterioration.
The person is forcibly confined in a prison-like psychiatric system.
The disability system determines the material resources available after discharge.
Police and emergency services may encounter the person during subsequent crises.
These institutions possess different statutory mandates.
The human being passes through all of them.
From the patient's perspective, the bureaucratic boundaries disappear.
There is simply a cycle.
Accordingly, Premier Doug Ford and the Government of Ontario properly form part of the political and systemic criticism
advanced here where provincial policy concerning disability assistance is being discussed.
The proposition is not that CAMH should receive inadequate funding because its patients are poor.
The proposition is the opposite:
A government capable of financing an enormous professional apparatus for psychiatric crisis should be capable of ensuring
that disabled people have realistic access to the food and shelter required for ordinary human stability.
It is perverse to accept extreme deprivation as an ordinary condition of disabled life and then spend enormously greater
resources managing the consequences after the person deteriorates.
14. Why This History Matters to the Present Dispute This history explains why contemporary interactions with CAMH cannot
be understood by treating CAMH merely as an ordinary service provider.
My concern about credibility, psychiatric characterization, work, independence and the involvement of police exists against a
history in which psychiatric characterization has had substantial consequences for my autonomy.
That history affects the significance of present events.
It also explains why evidence of functioning matters to me.
Work is not offered as magical proof that psychiatric illness does not exist.
A person with serious mental illness can work.
A person with serious mental illness can create.
A person with serious mental illness can operate sophisticated technology.
None of those propositions erases a diagnosis.
But sustained work can nevertheless constitute relevant evidence about functioning.
Long-term planning, sustained concentration, technical problem-solving, maintaining complicated projects, producing creative
work, communicating coherently about those projects, operating infrastructure and assuming continuing responsibilities can
reveal information about what a person is actually capable of doing.
The appropriate question is therefore not:
“Does this work prove that the patient has no psychiatric illness?”
It plainly does not.
The appropriate question is:
“If functioning is clinically relevant, why should substantial, objectively observable evidence of functioning receive no
consideration merely because that functioning occurs through creative or technical work?”
That distinction becomes important in the contemporary portions of this submission.
15. Institutional Power Requires Institutional Restraint Psychiatric institutions deal with people at moments when those
people may possess unusually little power.

Their words can be characterized as symptoms.
Their anger can be characterized as agitation.
Their resistance can be characterized as illness.
Their desperation can be recorded without its surrounding circumstances.
Police may become involved.
Liberty may be restricted.
Treatment decisions may follow.
None of this means psychiatric medicine is illegitimate.
It means that the greater the institution's power over an individual, the greater the importance of accuracy, context,
proportionality and individualized treatment.
A psychiatric diagnosis must never become an interpretive shortcut through which every disagreement proves illness, every
protest proves instability, every emotional reaction proves pathology, and contrary evidence of functioning becomes
invisible.
Nor should psychiatric diagnosis erase meaningful differences between patients.
I should be assessed according to my behaviour, my functioning, my history and my actual level of risk.
I should not be treated as interchangeable with a violent or profoundly unstable stranger merely because both of our medical
records contain psychiatric diagnoses.
And I should not be forced to endure terrorization and violence from other patients as though exposure to those conditions
were an inherent and unavoidable component of psychiatric treatment.
16. Conclusion: The System Surrounding the Incident The historical experiences described here are not included simply
because they were painful.
They establish the perspective from which the present dispute must be understood.
The issue is larger than a single unpleasant interaction.
It concerns a system in which poverty, disability assistance, psychiatric treatment, involuntary confinement, police
involvement and social marginalization can intersect in the life of the same person.
A government may regard disability benefits as one policy file.
A hospital may regard involuntary psychiatric treatment and confinement as a separate policy matter.
Police may regard crisis intervention as another.
But the patient experiences all three as one continuous life.
And there is a profound contradiction at the centre of that life.
The state can surround psychiatric disability with expensive institutions, professional salaries, physicians, nurses,
administrators, security, police intervention and emergency infrastructure while leaving the disabled person himself without
sufficient economic resources to reliably secure the ordinary necessities of human stability.
The machinery can prosper while the patient deteriorates.
The machinery can become most resource-rich precisely when the patient reaches his lowest point.
Then the machinery forcibly confines him in a prison-like system and returns him to poverty.
If deprivation contributes to another deterioration, the machinery returns.
That is the systemic cycle I intend to criticize.
It is why the comparative prosperity of the institutional apparatus is relevant.
It is why the poverty of the patient is relevant.
It is why food is relevant.
It is why confinement conditions are relevant.
It is why the differences between individual psychiatric patients are relevant.
And it is why the institutional interpretation of behaviour is relevant.
This is not an invitation to adjudicate every historical grievance.
It is an invitation to understand the system before judging what happened within it.
The patient is not merely the object upon which the mental-health system operates. The patient is the person the system
exists to serve. A system that preserves the prosperity and stability of the machinery while repeatedly returning its
supposed beneficiary to deprivation, instability and crisis deserves more than administrative acceptance. It deserves
scrutiny.

Legal Significance — CAMH
1. The Governing Human-Rights Framework Section 1 of Ontario's Human Rights Code guarantees equal treatment with
respect to services, goods and facilities without discrimination because of disability. Mental disorder falls expressly within
the Code's definition of disability. Section 8 separately protects the right to claim and enforce Code rights and to institute or
participate in Code proceedings without reprisal or threat of reprisal.
The Supreme Court of Canada has repeatedly emphasized that human-rights discrimination is concerned with substantive
adverse effects, not merely openly hostile intentions.

HRTO File 2026-67461-I | Written Submissions of the Applicant | Page 16

HUMAN RIGHTS TRIBUNAL OF ONTARIO

In Moore v. British Columbia (Education), 2012 SCC 61, the Supreme Court articulated the basic discrimination framework: a
claimant establishes prima facie discrimination by showing a protected characteristic, an adverse impact concerning the
relevant service, and that the protected characteristic was a factor in the adverse impact.
In Quebec (Commission des droits de la personne et des droits de la jeunesse) v. Bombardier Inc., 2015 SCC 39, the
Supreme Court confirmed that discrimination may be subtle and may arise through stereotypes or facially neutral
standards, while also emphasizing that the applicant must prove the connection between the protected ground and adverse
treatment on a balance of probabilities.
The significance here is that the Applicant need not prove personal hatred of persons with psychiatric disabilities.
The question is what happened, whether there was adverse treatment in the provision of services, and whether disability was
a factor in that treatment.
The Applicant's allegation is therefore not answered merely by saying that every decision was motivated by medical
“concern.”
Concern and discrimination are not mutually exclusive concepts.
A decision may be sincerely believed to protect a person and nevertheless impose an adverse effect connected to disability.
The evidence must determine which characterization is correct.
2. Psychiatric Disability Cannot Consume the Applicant's Entire Legal Identity The central factual allegation raises a
significant human-rights problem.
The Applicant alleges that his initiation of further human-rights proceedings became associated with psychiatric deterioration.
If proven, that association risks creating a circular disability stereotype:
the Applicant has psychiatric illness → the Applicant initiates contentious litigation → litigation is interpreted as evidence of
illness → psychiatric intervention follows or is threatened → exercising legal rights becomes dangerous for the Applicant.
The Code protects mental disability.
It also separately protects the exercise of Code rights.
Those protections would be severely weakened if the exercise of the second could itself routinely become evidence justifying
adverse intervention under the first.
This does not mean psychiatric professionals must ignore genuine symptoms merely because they occur while a patient is
engaged in litigation.
It means that the existence of litigation cannot substitute for an individualized assessment of actual psychiatric condition and
actual statutory criteria.
The Supreme Court's accommodation jurisprudence strongly favours individualized assessment over rules founded upon
assumptions about groups.
In British Columbia (Public Service Employee Relations Commission) v. BCGSEU, 1999 CanLII 652 (SCC) (“Meiorin”), the
Supreme Court required discriminatory standards to be justified as reasonably necessary and stressed accommodation
short of undue hardship.
In Quebec (CNESST) v. Caron, 2018 SCC 3, the Court again described accommodation as requiring reasonable and
practical measures capable of avoiding the negative impact on the individual, short of undue hardship.
Those cases arise in different service and employment contexts, but the underlying human-rights principle is important here:
disability requires attention to the actual individual, not merely assumptions attached to the diagnostic category.
3. The Reprisal Question Is Potentially Central — But Requires Proof of Intent Section 8 of the Code provides that every
person has the right to claim and enforce Code rights and institute and participate in Code proceedings without reprisal or
threat of reprisal.
Ontario authority gives this allegation a specific legal structure.
In Noble v. York University, 2010 HRTO 878, the Tribunal identified three elements:
1. an action or threat against the applicant;
2. a relationship between that action or threat and the applicant's attempt to claim or
enforce a Code right; and
3. an intention to retaliate for the exercise or attempted exercise of the Code right. The Tribunal has also recognized that an
applicant need not already have formally commenced a proceeding, and need not ultimately prove the underlying
discrimination complaint, in order for reprisal protection to operate.
This is important because it identifies both the potential strength and the principal evidentiary difficulty of this allegation.
The chronology alleged by the Applicant is potentially significant:
HRTO proceeding → communication concerning the proceeding → attempted disengagement from meetings → attempt to
obtain medication elsewhere → alleged blocking of medication → eventual return to the CAMH relationship.
Chronology alone, however, does not establish retaliatory intent.

6. Equal Access to Services Section 1 of the Code protects equal treatment with respect to services on the ground of
disability.
Moore is particularly instructive because the Supreme Court rejected an analysis that defined the service too narrowly. The
relevant service there was meaningful access to education, rather than merely the particular special- education program

Applied by analogy here, the relevant question should not necessarily be reduced to:
“Was the Applicant entitled to have this particular clinic administer this particular injection on his preferred terms?”
The larger service question may concern meaningful, non-discriminatory access to psychiatric healthcare and necessary
medication.
That distinction matters.
A technically available service may not constitute substantively equal access if obtaining it is conditioned upon requirements
that impose disability-related disadvantages which cannot be justified.
At the same time, legitimate clinical requirements remain legitimate. The Tribunal would therefore need evidence explaining
why the restriction existed and whether reasonable alternatives were available.

Implications and Appropriate Structural Remedies — CAMH STRUCTURAL REMEDIES
1. What These Allegations Imply If the evidence ultimately establishes the Applicant's account, the implications extend
beyond one disagreement between one patient and one psychiatrist.
The potential structural problem would be this:
A psychiatric patient must be able to exercise ordinary legal rights—including human-rights rights— without the exercise of
those rights itself becoming presumptive evidence of psychiatric deterioration.
Likewise:
A psychiatric patient who possesses legal capacity must have meaningful autonomy unless a lawful clinical or statutory basis
exists for restricting it.
And:
Access to medically necessary treatment should not be used as leverage to compel participation in a particular therapeutic
relationship unless the restriction is actually required by legitimate clinical considerations and complies with applicable law.
Those propositions do not require dismantling psychiatric medicine.
They require distinguishing treatment from coercion, clinical assessment from stereotype, and legitimate safety intervention
from adverse treatment connected to disability or the exercise of human-rights rights.
2. Are Grand Structural Changes Being Requested? Potentially, yes—but the requested changes must remain tethered to
proven Code infringements and to the respondents actually before the Tribunal.
The HRTO possesses substantial remedial authority.
Section 45.2 permits monetary compensation, non-monetary restitution, and—critically—an order directing a party to “do
anything” the Tribunal considers appropriate to promote compliance with the Code. The statute expressly provides that
such orders may govern future practices, even where the applicant did not originally request the particular futurecompliance order.
The Ontario Human Rights Commission describes public-interest remedies as potentially including changes to discriminatory
practices, monitoring, policies and widespread staff or management training.
That does not give the HRTO unlimited jurisdiction to redesign Ontario's healthcare system.
The Tribunal itself has emphasized that systemic remedies should be tied to the facts, remedial rather than punitive, clear
enough to enforce, directed toward Code compliance, and should not transform the Tribunal into an ongoing policymaker.
In the Ontario midwives litigation, the HRTO recognized that systemic discrimination can justify systemic remedies while
maintaining those limits.
Accordingly, the strongest structural requests arising from these allegations would not be:
“Rebuild CAMH according to the Applicant's preferred psychiatric philosophy.”
They would be targeted requirements such as:
Human-rights activity must not itself be treated as evidence of psychiatric instability absent independent individualized clinical
evidence.
CAMH should have a clear process ensuring that patients capable of making treatment decisions can seek reasonable
continuity of necessary medication when changing providers or declining a particular clinical relationship, subject to
legitimate medical and legal requirements.
Where a treatment restriction occurs shortly after protected human-rights activity, the legitimate clinical basis for that
restriction should be clearly documented.
Clinical decision-making should distinguish protected legal advocacy, disagreement and ordinary emotional responses from
symptoms of psychiatric pathology through individualized assessment rather than assumption.
Relevant evidence of a patient's actual functioning should be considered where functioning forms part of the clinical
assessment.

3. The Central Human-Rights Principle
The ultimate significance of these facts is autonomy.
A diagnosis of serious mental illness does not erase citizenship.
It does not erase the right to litigate.
It does not erase the right to disagree with a doctor.
It does not erase the right to change doctors or seek treatment elsewhere, subject to legitimate requirements governing the
treatment involved.
It does not transform every unusual decision into a symptom.
And it cannot mean that the machinery intended to protect a psychiatric patient may be invoked against him merely because
he exercises rights that those without psychiatric diagnoses exercise without fear of confinement.
The Human Rights Code protects the Applicant because he has a disability.
It would be a profound inversion of that protection if the disability itself became the mechanism through which exercising his
Code rights exposed him to greater institutional control.
That is the issue raised by these facts.
The Tribunal need not accept the Applicant's characterization merely because he sincerely believes it.
The Applicant must prove his case.
But CAMH likewise should not be permitted to answer the allegations merely by replacing the word “coercion” with “concern.”
The evidence must determine what occurred.
If necessary medication was restricted for legitimate and individualized medical reasons unrelated to protected activity, the
evidence should demonstrate that.
If psychiatric intervention was contemplated because independent evidence genuinely established deterioration or danger,
the evidence should demonstrate that.

At that point the case concerns not simply poor communication or a disagreement between doctor and patient.

That is properly a human-rights question.

THE FACTS OF THE MATTER - TPS

The Event-Specific TPS Allegation
My complaint concerning the Toronto Police Service is principally directed at the Hate Crimes Unit and the manner in which
my call was handled. The central point is simple: I called seeking to report what I believed were hate-motivated incidents
connected to a protected characteristic. Before I had a meaningful opportunity to explain the incidents, the available
identifying information, or why I believed disability was relevant, the officer had already begun arranging to transfer me out
of the Hate Crimes Unit.
The significance of the timing is not that an officer is required to remain on a telephone call for some arbitrary minimum
number of seconds. The significance is what had not yet been heard. The underlying account involved several incidents
that I had, at the time, partly combined in my own mind. Some were serious; some were less serious; and I have since
reconsidered or corrected aspects of my original interpretation. I needed time to separate them and explain them. Instead,
within approximately one minute, questions were already being asked for the purpose of transferring my call to a division.
The transfer process itself then took approximately another one to two minutes.
What I Had Not Yet Been Able to Explain
The police position appears to have been that I had not provided information capable of identifying a suspect. My objection is
that I had not yet been given a meaningful opportunity to provide it. I had a description relating to one person and a
photograph relating to another. We never reached a substantive discussion of that information before the transfer decision
was underway.
The incidents I was trying to explain included the following. I had experienced a longer pattern of people spitting or appearing
to spit near or at me. On one occasion, I believed someone had spat on my chest while I was travelling toward Moores in
the downtown financial district. I later reconsidered that event and accept that it is possible I spat on myself while coughing
after smoking. I had considered that possibility at the time and dismissed it too quickly. On another occasion, a man on a
bicycle spat on me. I acknowledge that I had been behaving in an annoying manner. I also previously described a young
man carrying a staff in a way that made him sound threatening; I later encountered him again and concluded that I had
unintentionally misrepresented him. He appeared to be a regular person in the neighbourhood and I no longer regard that
incident as evidence that I was in danger.
Another incident was different. I encountered an older Southeast Asian man whom I understood to work at a Pizza Hut. As I
walked by, he turned and spat in my direction. I moved and the spit did not land on me. I understood this as an attempted

evidence. I therefore had, at least in relation to that incident, a photograph and a place of work that could potentially assist
identification.
At the end of the factual account, I intended to explain why I believed the pattern was connected to mental illness as a
protected ground. That explanation was not simple. It involved the circumstances of my neighbourhood, my public creative

service I sought was redirected.
The Later Officer and an Important Concession
A police officer later attended and listened to my account. I found him professional, friendly and willing to hear me. We even
spoke about Judge Dredd comics. No report number was provided and, to my understanding, no formal report was

LEGAL SIGNIFICANCE - TPS

Legal Significance - Access to Police Services
Section 1 of Ontario's Human Rights Code protects equal treatment with respect to services without discrimination because
of, among other grounds, race and disability. The Applicant's allegation is therefore most precisely framed as a question of
equal access to public services: whether his psychiatric disability, race, or both were factors in the decision to redirect him
before the substance of his complaint had been meaningfully heard.
The Supreme Court of Canada's discrimination framework requires a protected characteristic, an adverse impact in the
relevant service, and proof that the protected characteristic was a factor in the adverse impact. Intent to discriminate is not
required. At the same time, the connection to the protected ground must be proved on a balance of probabilities; it cannot
rest on speculation alone. That makes the contemporaneous call recording, transfer records, officer evidence, dispatch
notes and applicable policies substantially more important than rhetoric about what the officer 'must' have thought.
Disability: The Stronger Evidentiary Theory
On the present record, disability is the more direct theory because the subsequent response expressly moved toward food,
shelter and wellness intervention rather than simply receiving the complainant as a person attempting to report an offence.
The Applicant alleges that his psychiatric presentation or disclosure caused the service to reclassify him from complainant
to person in need of crisis management before the underlying complaint had been heard.
The Supreme Court has recognized that discrimination may operate through perception, myths and stereotypes about
disability, not only through actual functional limitations. The question is therefore not whether the Applicant had a
psychiatric diagnosis - he did - but whether assumptions associated with that diagnosis materially affected the service he
received.
Race: Preserve the Pleading, Require the Proof
Race remains a pleaded ground, but the allegation must be advanced with evidentiary discipline. Ontario appellate authority
recognizes that discrimination is often proved circumstantially and that an adjudicator may draw reasonable inferences
from multiple pieces of evidence. It is equally important, however, not to reverse the burden by assuming discrimination
merely because the decision is unexplained. The Applicant should therefore invite the Tribunal to examine whether race
was a factor, rather than state as an established fact that it was.
The more rigorous formulation does not depend upon asserting that only two logically possible explanations existed for the
transfer. Other explanations are possible. The evidentiary question is narrower and stronger: if TPS says neither race nor
disability played any role, what contemporaneous, non-discriminatory reason explains the decision to transfer the call
before the substance of the complaint had been heard? The Respondent can answer that question with evidence. The
Tribunal can then assess that explanation against the recording and the surrounding circumstances.
Reprisal
The Applicant also raises reprisal. That allegation should be kept analytically separate from the original transfer. The original
transfer occurred before the later assertion that an HRTO complaint would be made and therefore cannot itself have been
retaliation for that later assertion. The potential reprisal issue concerns what happened after the Applicant invoked humanrights protections and announced an intention to complain, including whether the wellness response was initiated,
escalated or maintained with retaliatory intent.
Because reprisal under section 8 requires proof of retaliatory intention, chronology is relevant but not enough by itself. The
strongest evidence would be the recordings and records showing what was said immediately before the wellness response,
HRTO File 2026-67461-I | Written Submissions of the Applicant | Page 24

HUMAN RIGHTS TRIBUNAL OF ONTARIO

who initiated it, the reasons recorded for it, whether the response changed after HRTO was mentioned, and whether the
officers or dispatchers understood the Applicant to be exercising or threatening to exercise Code rights.
What the Tribunal Is and Is Not Asked to Decide
The Tribunal need not determine whether every incident the Applicant originally attributed to hate independently satisfied the
legal characterization of a hate-motivated offence. Nor is it asked to conclude that the later attending officer was wrong not
to file a report. The narrower issue is whether the Applicant received equal treatment in access to the police service at the
point when the Hate Crimes Unit decided to redirect him.
This distinction protects both sides of the case. Police remain free to assess credibility, jurisdiction, evidentiary sufficiency
and whether an incident is actionable. The Applicant, however, says those judgments should follow a meaningful
opportunity to present the relevant facts, rather than be replaced by disability-based assumptions about what kind of
service a psychiatric complainant requires.
Evidence That Should Resolve the Dispute
The most probative evidence is likely to include: the complete Hate Crimes Unit call recording; the time stamps showing
when the transfer decision was initiated; dispatch and transfer notes; any occurrence or CAD records; the identities and
testimony of the involved personnel; TPS policies governing Hate Crimes Unit intake and transfers; records concerning the
proposed wellness check; the later attending officer's notes; and the Applicant's contemporaneous email chain containing
the photograph and other identifying information.
This evidence permits the Tribunal to adjudicate the dispute on the contemporaneous record rather than on either party's
characterization alone. It can determine what the officer knew at the moment of transfer, what information had not yet been
heard, why the call was redirected, and whether a protected ground was a factor.
