Introduction

This supplementary submission is filed to address the disproportionate impact of the Respondent’s policies on the Applicant and to place relevant personal history before the Tribunal for the proper assessment of remedy. The Applicant’s claim centres on adverse-effects discrimination arising from the Respondent’s treatment of artificial-intelligence tools used as a disability-related accommodation. The present submission focuses on the heightened vulnerability of the Applicant and the consequent severity of the economic exclusion he has experienced.

Argument

The Applicant lives well below the deep poverty line. His only independent income is Ontario Disability Support Program benefits, which are funded at approximately half the level required for basic subsistence. He relies on residual family support through his uncle, who holds power of attorney for his mother, who lives with dementia. His income from all independent sources is zero.

In this context, the denial of monetization for works created with artificial intelligence as a disability aid does not merely deprive the Applicant of discretionary revenue. It imposes and perpetuates a state of absolute financial precarity. The same quantum of lost income that might represent an inconvenience to a person of average means constitutes, for the Applicant, the difference between bare survival and the possibility of minimal stability.

Canadian law requires that a respondent take the claimant as it finds him. The Supreme Court of Canada in Athey v. Leonati, [1996] 3 S.C.R. 458, articulated the thin-skull (or eggshell-skull) principle as follows: the wrongdoer is liable for the full extent of the harm caused, even where that harm is unexpectedly severe because of a pre-existing vulnerability or condition. The Court stated that the tortfeasor “must take his or her victim as the tortfeasor finds the victim, and is therefore liable even though the plaintiff’s losses are more dramatic than they would be for the average person.”

Although they arose in the tort context, the underlying principle informs the assessment of damages under the Ontario Human Rights Code. The Tribunal consistently treats an applicant’s particular vulnerability—including pre-existing disability, trauma, and extreme financial precarity—as a relevant factor when quantifying both economic loss and compensation for injury to dignity, feelings, and self-respect.

The Applicant’s history demonstrates precisely such vulnerability. He has previously experienced prolonged periods of near-starvation when deprived of income, resulting in physical collapse so severe that recovery required many months. Those episodes of extreme deprivation have, in the past, precipitated mental-health crises, institutionalization, the loss of housing, the breakdown of his marriage, the forced adoption of beloved pets, and other irreversible personal losses. On one occasion, inability to afford even basic transportation led to an interruption in medication, which in turn contributed to a serious mental-health episode and the permanent loss of his family home.

This history is not recited for its emotional force alone. It establishes that the Applicant is a person for whom the sudden or prolonged removal of income-generating capacity carries consequences far beyond those that would be experienced by a neurotypical person of average means. The Respondent’s policy of denying monetization to works created with artificial intelligence as a disability aid therefore lands upon a thin skull. The economic exclusion is not an abstract or average harm; it is a harm that, given the Applicant’s documented vulnerabilities, threatens the very limited stability he has managed to achieve.

The Applicant acknowledges that the platform itself has, in other respects, contributed positively to his recent recovery and artistic development. He does not seek a hostile confrontation. He seeks only recognition that the specific policy at issue produces a discriminatory and disproportionately severe impact upon him as a person with a disability who relies upon artificial intelligence as an accommodation, and who already lives at the outermost margin of financial survival.

Conclusion

The thin-skull principle, as stated by the Supreme Court of Canada in Athey v. Leonati, [1996] 3 S.C.R. 458, requires that the Respondent take the Applicant as it finds him. The Applicant’s history of near-fatal deprivation, mental-health crises triggered by poverty, and irreversible personal losses establishes a heightened vulnerability. The ongoing denial of monetization for disability-related artificial-intelligence works therefore causes a more severe form of economic and dignitary harm than the same policy would cause to a person without that history.

The Applicant respectfully requests that the Tribunal take this vulnerability into account when assessing both liability for adverse-effects discrimination and the appropriate quantum of any monetary and non-monetary remedies.

The Applicant remains open to a constructive and mutually beneficial resolution.
