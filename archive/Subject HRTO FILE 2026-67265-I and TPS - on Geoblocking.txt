"In Canadian human rights law, an employer cannot dictate the brand or model of your personal wheelchair.
Because a personal wheelchair is a medically prescribed device required both inside and outside the workplace, forcing an
employee to change it would violate provincial human rights codes (such as the Ontario Human Rights Code) or the federal
Canadian Human Rights Act. [1]
The legal framework and Supreme Court precedents that prevent an employer from controlling your personal medical equipment
rest on three distinct legal pillars:
1. The Meiorin Test (Bona Fide Occupational Requirement)
The absolute bedrock of accommodation law in Canada is the Supreme Court of Canada landmark decision British Columbia
(Public Service Employee Relations Commission) v. BCGSEU [1999] 3 S.C.R. 3 (commonly known as the "Meiorin" case).
[1]
The Precedent: Under the Meiorin test, if an employer sets a rule (like "you must use X brand of wheelchair"), they bear
the burden of proving that the rule is a Bona Fide Occupational Requirement (BFOR). [1]
To do this, the employer must prove that it is scientifically or operationally impossible to accommodate your specific
wheelchair brand without causing the business undue hardship (which is a very high financial or safety threshold in
Canada). A preference for a different brand, aesthetic uniformity, or minor inconvenience never passes the Meiorin test.
[1, 2]
2. The Individualized Assessment Rule
The Supreme Court of Canada reinforced in Central Okanagan School District No. 23 v. Renaud [1992] 2 S.C.R. 970 that the
duty to accommodate requires an "individualized assessment". [1, 2]
The Precedent: Accommodation must be tailored to the unique physical requirements of the specific person. Because a
wheelchair brand and configuration are chosen by medical professionals based on your specific body dimensions,
pressure-sore risks, and functional needs, the employer has no medical authority to alter that prescription. Human rights
tribunals consistently rule that an employer must defer to your treating physician's equipment recommendations. [1, 2]

3. The Duty to Modify the Workplace (Not the Person)
Under Canadian jurisprudence, the legal obligation of the employer is to modify the workplace environment, not the individual or
their personal mobility aids. [1, 2]
The Precedent: If your wheelchair doesn't fit standard cubicles or carpeted areas, provincial bodies like the Ontario
Human Rights Commission (OHRC) mandate that the employer must alter the physical office spaces (e.g., reconfiguring
paths, adjusting desk heights, or changing flooring) to ensure full access. Demanding that you change your personal
medical device to fit an inaccessible environment is considered direct discrimination based on disability. [1, 2, 3]
If your employer or HR department is making this demand, you can formally request their reasons in writing and remind them of
their procedural duty to accommodate you to the point of undue hardship. [1, 2]

An item counts as a legally protected disability aid based on the following framework:
1. Acceptance in "Good Faith"
The Canadian Human Rights Commission (CHRC) and provincial tribunals explicitly state that employers must accept
accommodation requests in good faith. If it is visually obvious or logically clear that you require a wheelchair or assistive device
to move around, an employer cannot deny your right to use it simply because you haven't shown them a prescription slip. [1]
2. The Medical Confirmation Threshold
While you do not need a prescription to start using an aid, an employer is allowed to ask for medical documentation if your
disability is non-evident, or if they need to verify your specific functional limitations to build a workplace accommodation plan.
[1]
However, this documentation does not need to be a prescription. A simple medical note or functional abilities form from your
doctor, physiotherapist, or occupational therapist stating that you "require a manual/power wheelchair for daily mobility" is more
than enough.
