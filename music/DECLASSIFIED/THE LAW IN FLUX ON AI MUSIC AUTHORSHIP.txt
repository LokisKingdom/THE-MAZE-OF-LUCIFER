Feeding someone else's copyrighted lyrics into an AI to generate a song is an infringing work, while feeding your own lyrics into an AI gives you ownership of your lyrics, but the purely AI-generated musical accompaniment remains uncopyrightable and falls into the public domain.
Scenario A: Someone Else Feeds Your Lyrics Into an AI
• Infringement Occurs: If a third party takes your protected lyrics and inputs them into a generative AI tool (like Suno or Udio) to make a track, they are still reproducing and creating an unauthorized derivative work of your copyrighted text.
• No Legal Shield: Using an AI program as the "middleman" does not excuse copyright infringement. The resulting file still incorporates your exclusive literary/musical composition without permission.
Scenario B: You Feed Your Own Lyrics Into an AI
• You Own the Lyrics: Because you wrote the words, you retain full copyright ownership over the underlying text/lyrics.
• The AI Music is Unprotectable: Under the human authorship requirement enforced by copyright offices (such as the U.S. Copyright Office and general Canadian copyright principles), purely automated, prompt-generated music lacks human creative input and cannot be copyrighted. It enters the public domain.
• Hybrid Protection: If you significantly edit the AI output, add human-played instruments, or compose your own melodies alongside the tool's arrangements, you can claim a copyright on the human-modified parts while disclaiming the pure AI portions.
Is Infringement by a Third Party Required to Gain Ownership?
• No Connection: Infringement by a third party has no bearing on whether you own a copyright. Copyright ownership is established entirely by independent human creation, original expression, and fixation in a tangible medium. Someone else stealing or infringing upon your work does not grant you ownership of an AI track, nor does a lack of infringement mean you automatically own machine-generated notes.

1. Why the Infringer's Work Is Infringing (Not Just "Non-Copyrightable")
The infringer's AI song is declared non-copyrightable—the infringer owns absolutely nothing. However, because it contains your stolen lyrics, it is also legally classified as an infringing work.
• The Law Sees a Copy: If someone steals your lyrics and pastes them into an AI, the AI output literally contains your copyrighted text. The law doesn't care that a machine generated the music; it cares that your protected words were used without permission.
• Infringement Doesn't Require Human Music: If someone copies your book chapters and uses an AI to read them aloud in a robotic voice, they have infringed your copyright. The fact that a machine made the audio doesn't erase the theft of the text.
2. How "Lacks Human Input" Is Determined for the Music
When the U.S. Copyright Office or courts determine if a work "lacks human input," they look at who controlled the specific expression of the element being registered.
When you or an infringer type a prompt or paste lyrics into a music AI, a clear legal line is drawn between the lyrics and the music:
Element of the Song	Who Made It?	Is There Human Input?	Copyright Status
The Lyrics	You (Years ago)	Yes (Human-created text)	Fully Copyrighted by You.
The Music / Melody / Audio	The AI	No (Machine-generated audio)	Public Domain (Uncopyrightable).

You did not choose the specific notes, chords, tempo, or vocal pitches; the AI's algorithm randomized and generated them. Because no human actively composed the audio track, the audio track itself cannot be copyrighted by anyone.

2. They own nothing, because the music is AI-generated and the lyrics are stolen.
