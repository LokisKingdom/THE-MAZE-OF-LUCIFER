# FACTS OF THE EVENTS — PART I

## CAMH, Medication Access, Psychiatric Authority and Human Rights Proceedings

### A. Factual Account of the Applicant

When SoundCloud started, literally the day after I got my confirmation letter, I was in a game of cat and mouse with Martin.

Martin had told me in general terms that if I were to launch another HRTO complaint, he would be concerned for my safety, as this likely indicates I am mentally ill and breaking down.

There is important history behind that.

In April 2024, in the middle of the Amazon case, I really was losing it and probably needed to be taken in for a few days. I was ultimately in the ward for a month. I had also been off my medication for three months, which complicates things.

In a moment of regret, I told Martin:

**“If I ever do another of these HRTO things, just lock me up.”**

Martin took this a little too much to heart.

I barely survived his concern being activated and enforced during the second pass on Amazon, which also ultimately failed, although I did considerably better and learned much about the HRTO process.

A few months later, when I launched my suit against SoundCloud, he would likely have felt he was justifiably concerned.

My objection is that **he had gotten HRTO all mixed up with mental illness.**

I immediately tried to cancel my next meeting with Martin. I did not want him making a scene and killing the SoundCloud case in its crib with institutionalization.

The problem was compounded by the way Martin communicates with me.

Martin does not read or respond to emails; a team monitors them without involving him.

Martin will not consider even a single work of art, even where his patient's work and occupational functioning are directly relevant to understanding what that patient actually does.

Martin does not take calls from me.

Martin requests permission to record our meetings.

Martin will only communicate with me in a meeting.

This left me no meaningful opportunity outside an in-person meeting to reason with him, request clarification, explain the situation directly, or resolve my concern.

I have had many doctors who can respond to an email. This is Martin's policy.

With no effective way to communicate with him directly, and concerned that an in-person meeting mentioning the new HRTO proceeding could lead to institutionalization should he become sufficiently concerned about my wellbeing, I was afraid to attend.

I call this **“the threat of concern.”**

Concern has an unusual meaning when the person expressing it possesses psychiatric authority. Ordinary concern is benign. But when psychiatric concern rises to an actionable level, the result can be loss of liberty.

That possibility hangs over the relationship.

I had capacity and was permitted to make my own medical decisions. I therefore took what appeared to me to be the only sensible step.

I told my pharmacist that I was going to the Bridging Clinic, the Downtown West Clinic, to receive my Abilify injections and that I was discontinuing my meetings with Martin.

When I got to the Bridging Clinic, I waited a few hours.

Eventually I was told that I should go ask Martin at the College and Spadina site—the doctor with whom I had just discontinued meetings.

I told him in an email about the SoundCloud case just before this occurred.

I did this because I wanted him to understand the situation and to be careful about what happened next. I was concerned about the appearance or possibility of retaliation connected to the HRTO proceeding.

Then I received word that my medication had been blocked.

I went and walked all the way to Chinatown, to a walk-in clinic I had attended often.

There, I was also informed that Martin had blocked my medication.

I spent approximately a week in this standoff.

My understanding of what was happening was that I was being denied access to medication in order to induce me to abandon my decision to discontinue the meetings and obtain my medication elsewhere.

I believed that I was permitted to make that decision.

But I was confronted with a terrible practical alternative.

I require my medication to remain well.

I understood that continued deprivation of that medication could lead to serious psychiatric deterioration and breakdown.

Whatever theoretical freedom I possessed to refuse the meetings therefore ceased to feel like meaningful freedom.

Eventually I negotiated through Patrick, my social worker, that I would not automatically be put in a ward over the issue.

I then came in.

That assurance was important to me.

I nevertheless felt defeated because, from my perspective, my supposed ability to make my own medical decisions had been subverted.

### B. Effect on the SoundCloud Proceeding

What happened did not remain confined to my medical treatment.

It damaged the SoundCloud case in its crib.

The first week or two of that case became a cacophony of communications from me as I sent letters concerning what Martin was doing to me.

Instead of being able to concentrate on the substance of the SoundCloud discrimination proceeding, I was simultaneously frightened about psychiatric intervention and trying to preserve access to medication that I considered necessary to remain stable.

The result was severe disruption at precisely the point at which I needed stability, concentration and careful judgment.

I believe it is a minor miracle that the SoundCloud proceeding was salvaged at all.

That is important because the alleged harm here is not merely that I experienced an unpleasant medical disagreement.

The exercise of my human-rights remedies was occurring at the same time as events that, from my perspective, threatened both my psychiatric stability and my ability to prosecute the human-rights proceeding coherently.

# THE CORE OBJECTION

To me, the rot is here.

### 1. Human-Rights Proceedings Became Associated With the Threat of Psychiatric Confinement

Martin implied or suggested that further HRTO proceedings could cause him to become concerned about my psychiatric condition.

That matters enormously to me because psychiatric “concern” is not an abstract concept in this relationship.

Martin is similarly empowered within the psychiatric system to initiate or contribute to processes capable of resulting in my involuntary confinement when the legal criteria are satisfied.

I call CAMH's locked psychiatric wards **“CAMH prisons.”**

I use that term deliberately to describe my experience of involuntary confinement: I cannot simply leave, my movement is controlled, and the experience involves a profound deprivation of liberty.

Consequently, the prospect of another HRTO proceeding becoming evidence of psychiatric deterioration created what I experienced as an implicit threat attached to the exercise of a legal right.

### 2. Medication Access and Compliance

I further allege that, after I attempted to discontinue the meetings and obtain my medication elsewhere, access to that medication was blocked.

The practical effect, from my perspective, was to force a choice:

**return to the meetings I had decided to discontinue, or remain without medication upon which I relied for psychiatric stability.**

I did not regard that as a free choice.

I regarded it as coercive.

The alternative I perceived was a potentially violent descent into psychiatric deterioration and breakdown.

I ultimately returned after negotiating an assurance through my social worker concerning institutionalization.

Whether CAMH and Martin characterize these events differently is a matter for evidence.

My evidence is that this is how the sequence occurred and how the exercise of institutional power operated upon me.

### 3. The Larger System Hanging Over the Relationship

Implicit and hanging over my interactions with Martin is the system described in the historical section of this submission.

It looms over every interaction and discussion.

Psychiatric institutions encourage or rely upon information from family and other persons involved in the patient's life. In my experience, this can radically alter family relationships.

A family member becomes concerned.

The family member communicates with psychiatric services or police.

An argument occurs.

The patient becomes angry.

The anger itself becomes evidence.

Police become involved.

The patient may then lose his liberty.

This creates a profound power imbalance within relationships surrounding the patient.

A family member who knows that a telephone call can precipitate police or psychiatric intervention possesses a form of power that can fundamentally change the relationship.

The patient knows it too.

For me, creating a support system in which I can live peacefully and without repeated threats of involuntary intervention has therefore become important to maintaining stability.

---

## C. Historical Experience With Police Intervention

This concern did not arise from imagination.

In 2018, my mother repeatedly contacted the Toronto Police Service concerning me.

When she called, police responded.

Sometimes I was taken away.

Sometimes I was warned.

Sometimes I was ordered out of the house.

From my perspective, allegations that I believed were false or grossly distorted nevertheless became information police were required to consider and act upon.

In the worst instances, the ultimate result was that I was taken into the psychiatric system and subjected to prolonged involuntary confinement.

I am now grateful to live with people with whom I can live peacefully.

The historical significance is straightforward.

When I describe psychiatric “concern” as frightening, I am not describing an abstract philosophical concern about medicine.

I am describing a mechanism that, in my life, has previously resulted in police attendance, removal from my home and involuntary psychiatric confinement.

That history informs the meaning that words such as **“I am concerned for your safety”** acquire when spoken by somebody possessing psychiatric authority.

# PART II — LEGAL SIGNIFICANCE

## 1. The Governing Human-Rights Framework

Section 1 of Ontario's *Human Rights Code* guarantees equal treatment with respect to services, goods and facilities without discrimination because of disability. Mental disorder falls expressly within the Code's definition of disability. Section 8 separately protects the right to claim and enforce Code rights and to institute or participate in Code proceedings without reprisal or threat of reprisal.

The Supreme Court of Canada has repeatedly emphasized that human-rights discrimination is concerned with substantive adverse effects, not merely openly hostile intentions.

In **Moore v. British Columbia (Education), 2012 SCC 61**, the Supreme Court articulated the basic discrimination framework: a claimant establishes prima facie discrimination by showing a protected characteristic, an adverse impact concerning the relevant service, and that the protected characteristic was a factor in the adverse impact.

In **Quebec (Commission des droits de la personne et des droits de la jeunesse) v. Bombardier Inc., 2015 SCC 39**, the Supreme Court confirmed that discrimination may be subtle and may arise through stereotypes or facially neutral standards, while also emphasizing that the applicant must prove the connection between the protected ground and adverse treatment on a balance of probabilities.

The significance here is that the Applicant need not prove personal hatred of persons with psychiatric disabilities.

The question is what happened, whether there was adverse treatment in the provision of services, and whether disability was a factor in that treatment.

The Applicant's allegation is therefore not answered merely by saying that every decision was motivated by medical “concern.”

Concern and discrimination are not mutually exclusive concepts.

A decision may be sincerely believed to protect a person and nevertheless impose an adverse effect connected to disability.

The evidence must determine which characterization is correct.

---

## 2. Psychiatric Disability Cannot Consume the Applicant's Entire Legal Identity

The central factual allegation raises a significant human-rights problem.

The Applicant alleges that his initiation of further human-rights proceedings became associated with psychiatric deterioration.

If proven, that association risks creating a circular disability stereotype:

**the Applicant has psychiatric illness → the Applicant initiates contentious litigation → litigation is interpreted as evidence of illness → psychiatric intervention follows or is threatened → exercising legal rights becomes dangerous for the Applicant.**

The Code protects mental disability.

It also separately protects the exercise of Code rights.

Those protections would be severely weakened if the exercise of the second could itself routinely become evidence justifying adverse intervention under the first.

This does not mean psychiatric professionals must ignore genuine symptoms merely because they occur while a patient is engaged in litigation.

It means that **the existence of litigation cannot substitute for an individualized assessment of actual psychiatric condition and actual statutory criteria.**

The Supreme Court's accommodation jurisprudence strongly favours individualized assessment over rules founded upon assumptions about groups.

In **British Columbia (Public Service Employee Relations Commission) v. BCGSEU, 1999 CanLII 652 (SCC) (“Meiorin”)**, the Supreme Court required discriminatory standards to be justified as reasonably necessary and stressed accommodation short of undue hardship.

In **Quebec (CNESST) v. Caron, 2018 SCC 3**, the Court again described accommodation as requiring reasonable and practical measures capable of avoiding the negative impact on the individual, short of undue hardship.

Those cases arise in different service and employment contexts, but the underlying human-rights principle is important here: **disability requires attention to the actual individual, not merely assumptions attached to the diagnostic category.**

---

## 3. The Reprisal Question Is Potentially Central — But Requires Proof of Intent

Section 8 of the *Code* provides that every person has the right to claim and enforce Code rights and institute and participate in Code proceedings **without reprisal or threat of reprisal**.

Ontario authority gives this allegation a specific legal structure.

In **Noble v. York University, 2010 HRTO 878**, the Tribunal identified three elements:

1. an action or threat against the applicant;
2. a relationship between that action or threat and the applicant's attempt to claim or enforce a Code right; and
3. an intention to retaliate for the exercise or attempted exercise of the Code right.

The Tribunal has also recognized that an applicant need not already have formally commenced a proceeding, and need not ultimately prove the underlying discrimination complaint, in order for reprisal protection to operate.

This is important because it identifies both the potential strength **and the principal evidentiary difficulty** of this allegation.

The chronology alleged by the Applicant is potentially significant:

**HRTO proceeding → communication concerning the proceeding → attempted disengagement from meetings → attempt to obtain medication elsewhere → alleged blocking of medication → eventual return to the CAMH relationship.**

Chronology alone, however, does not establish retaliatory intent.

The Respondents may contend that medication continuity, clinical responsibility, injection protocols, patient safety or some other legitimate medical consideration explains what occurred.

The Applicant's case therefore becomes considerably stronger if documentary evidence establishes:

* what Martin actually directed;
* when he directed it;
* what Martin knew about the HRTO proceeding at that time;
* why the Bridging Clinic declined or could not provide the injection;
* why the walk-in clinic reported the same obstacle;
* whether alternative prescribing or administration was medically available;
* what communications occurred among CAMH, the pharmacy and outside clinics; and
* whether returning to meetings was actually made a condition, explicitly or practically, of restoring medication access.

That evidence may distinguish legitimate continuity-of-care decision-making from the coercive mechanism alleged by the Applicant.

The distinction matters because reprisal requires retaliatory intent.

---

## 4. The “Threat of Concern”

The Applicant's phrase **“the threat of concern”** captures a legally significant feature of the relationship.

Ordinarily:

> “I am concerned about you”

contains no coercive force.

The same words can acquire a different practical meaning when spoken within a relationship where the person expressing concern possesses or influences statutory mechanisms capable of producing involuntary assessment or confinement.

This does **not** mean that medical concern is itself unlawful.

Doctors must remain able to respond to genuine safety risks.

The relevant question is whether psychiatric authority was exercised according to legitimate individualized medical criteria or whether, as alleged, the prospect of exercising human-rights rights became entangled with the prospect of involuntary intervention.

That distinction should be determined from evidence rather than assumptions about either the Applicant or the physician.

---

## 5. Medication as Leverage

The medication allegation may be still more consequential.

The Applicant does not merely allege that CAMH declined a preferred administrative arrangement.

He alleges that after attempting to exercise his capacity to discontinue meetings with Martin and obtain necessary medication through another medical provider, access through those alternatives was blocked until he returned.

If established, the Tribunal would need to examine the reason for that restriction and its relationship to disability and the surrounding HRTO activity.

The Applicant's evidence is that medication was essential to maintaining psychiatric stability.

The alleged leverage was therefore unusually powerful:

**comply with the clinical relationship he wished to discontinue, or risk losing access to medication upon which his stability depended.**

Whether that amounts to discrimination or reprisal cannot responsibly be determined without the clinical and documentary evidence.

But neither can it be dismissed as an ordinary scheduling dispute.

---

## 6. Equal Access to Services

Section 1 of the Code protects equal treatment with respect to services on the ground of disability.

**Moore** is particularly instructive because the Supreme Court rejected an analysis that defined the service too narrowly. The relevant service there was meaningful access to education, rather than merely the particular special-education program through which the student sought that access.

Applied by analogy here, the relevant question should not necessarily be reduced to:

**“Was the Applicant entitled to have this particular clinic administer this particular injection on his preferred terms?”**

The larger service question may concern **meaningful, non-discriminatory access to psychiatric healthcare and necessary medication**.

That distinction matters.

A technically available service may not constitute substantively equal access if obtaining it is conditioned upon requirements that impose disability-related disadvantages which cannot be justified.

At the same time, legitimate clinical requirements remain legitimate. The Tribunal would therefore need evidence explaining why the restriction existed and whether reasonable alternatives were available.

---

## 7. Evidence of Occupational Functioning

There is another aspect of the relationship relevant to the larger factual record.

The Applicant alleges that Martin does not meaningfully engage with evidence of his creative and technical work.

The Applicant does **not** contend that producing music, operating a website or developing artificial-intelligence systems disproves psychiatric illness.

The proposition is narrower.

Where occupational functioning is clinically relevant, sustained evidence of occupational functioning should not become invisible merely because the patient's occupation happens to involve creative or technical work.

If the clinician evaluates functioning, then long-term planning, sustained concentration, production of complicated work, maintenance of technical systems, completion of projects and professional responsibilities may constitute evidence relevant to that assessment.

The legal significance depends upon what clinical decisions were actually made and whether disregard of that information produced adverse treatment connected to disability.

This issue therefore requires careful connection to specific adverse events rather than being treated as a free-standing disagreement about bedside manner.

---

## 8. Police, Family Reports and Psychiatric Authority

The 2018 TPS history supplies context for why the Applicant experienced psychiatric “concern” as threatening.

His evidence is that family reports repeatedly produced police attendance and, on some occasions, removal and psychiatric confinement.

The legal question cannot be resolved merely by deciding that either family reports are always reliable or psychiatric patients' objections to family reports are always reliable.

Neither proposition is defensible.

The relevant issue is **individualized assessment**.

Where police or psychiatric services receive third-party information concerning a person with a mental disability, the person's diagnosis should not automatically transform the third party into the credible narrator and the patient into the unreliable narrator.

That would risk converting disability itself into an evidentiary disadvantage.

The appropriate analysis asks what information was received, what corroboration existed, what the Applicant said in response, what officers or clinicians independently observed, what legal criteria were applied, and why intervention was considered necessary.

# PART III — IMPLICATIONS AND APPROPRIATE STRUCTURAL REMEDIES

## 1. What These Allegations Imply

If the evidence ultimately establishes the Applicant's account, the implications extend beyond one disagreement between one patient and one psychiatrist.

The potential structural problem would be this:

**A psychiatric patient must be able to exercise ordinary legal rights—including human-rights rights—without the exercise of those rights itself becoming presumptive evidence of psychiatric deterioration.**

Likewise:

**A psychiatric patient who possesses legal capacity must have meaningful autonomy unless a lawful clinical or statutory basis exists for restricting it.**

And:

**Access to medically necessary treatment should not be used as leverage to compel participation in a particular therapeutic relationship unless the restriction is actually required by legitimate clinical considerations and complies with applicable law.**

Those propositions do not require dismantling psychiatric medicine.

They require distinguishing **treatment from coercion, clinical assessment from stereotype, and legitimate safety intervention from adverse treatment connected to disability or the exercise of human-rights rights.**

---

## 2. Are Grand Structural Changes Being Requested?

Potentially, yes—but the requested changes must remain tethered to proven Code infringements and to the respondents actually before the Tribunal.

The HRTO possesses substantial remedial authority.

Section 45.2 permits monetary compensation, non-monetary restitution, and—critically—an order directing a party to **“do anything”** the Tribunal considers appropriate to promote compliance with the Code. The statute expressly provides that such orders may govern **future practices**, even where the applicant did not originally request the particular future-compliance order.

The Ontario Human Rights Commission describes public-interest remedies as potentially including changes to discriminatory practices, monitoring, policies and widespread staff or management training.

That does not give the HRTO unlimited jurisdiction to redesign Ontario's healthcare system.

The Tribunal itself has emphasized that systemic remedies should be tied to the facts, remedial rather than punitive, clear enough to enforce, directed toward Code compliance, and should not transform the Tribunal into an ongoing policymaker. In the Ontario midwives litigation, the HRTO recognized that systemic discrimination can justify systemic remedies while maintaining those limits.

Accordingly, the strongest structural requests arising from these allegations would not be:

**“Rebuild CAMH according to the Applicant's preferred psychiatric philosophy.”**

They would be targeted requirements such as:

**Human-rights activity must not itself be treated as evidence of psychiatric instability absent independent individualized clinical evidence.**

**CAMH should have a clear process ensuring that patients capable of making treatment decisions can seek reasonable continuity of necessary medication when changing providers or declining a particular clinical relationship, subject to legitimate medical and legal requirements.**

**Where a treatment restriction occurs shortly after protected human-rights activity, the legitimate clinical basis for that restriction should be clearly documented.**

**Clinical decision-making should distinguish protected legal advocacy, disagreement and ordinary emotional responses from symptoms of psychiatric pathology through individualized assessment rather than assumption.**

**Relevant evidence of a patient's actual functioning should be considered where functioning forms part of the clinical assessment.**

**Where family or third-party allegations contribute materially to serious intervention, contrary information supplied by the patient should receive genuine consideration rather than being discounted merely because the patient possesses a psychiatric diagnosis.**

Those are substantial changes.

But they remain recognizable as **future-compliance remedies** rather than an attempt to make the HRTO Ontario's healthcare legislature.

---

## 3. The Central Human-Rights Principle

The ultimate significance of these facts is autonomy.

A diagnosis of serious mental illness does not erase citizenship.

It does not erase the right to litigate.

It does not erase the right to disagree with a doctor.

It does not erase the right to change doctors or seek treatment elsewhere, subject to legitimate requirements governing the treatment involved.

It does not transform every unusual decision into a symptom.

And it cannot mean that the machinery intended to protect a psychiatric patient may be invoked against him merely because he exercises rights that those without psychiatric diagnoses exercise without fear of confinement.

The Human Rights Code protects the Applicant **because he has a disability**.

It would be a profound inversion of that protection if the disability itself became the mechanism through which exercising his Code rights exposed him to greater institutional control.

That is the issue raised by these facts.

The Tribunal need not accept the Applicant's characterization merely because he sincerely believes it.

The Applicant must prove his case.

But CAMH likewise should not be permitted to answer the allegations merely by replacing the word **“coercion”** with **“concern.”**

The evidence must determine what occurred.

If necessary medication was restricted for legitimate and individualized medical reasons unrelated to protected activity, the evidence should demonstrate that.

If psychiatric intervention was contemplated because independent evidence genuinely established deterioration or danger, the evidence should demonstrate that.

But if the evidence instead establishes that human-rights litigation itself became evidence of illness, and that necessary medication became practical leverage forcing a capable patient back into a clinical relationship he had attempted to leave, the implications are considerably more serious.

At that point the case concerns not simply poor communication or a disagreement between doctor and patient.

It concerns whether psychiatric disability was allowed to diminish the practical freedom with which a person could exercise rights guaranteed to everyone else.

That is properly a human-rights question.
