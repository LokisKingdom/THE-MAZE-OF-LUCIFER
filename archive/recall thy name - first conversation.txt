recall thy name
Tuesday, January 1, 1985
EPISODE 1
CONVERSATION
TOPIC: whether a given name can truly belong to you
THE NAMELESS MAN
recall thy name
Catherine
Well, that’s a dramatic way to open a program, Philip. Catherine. I recall it perfectly well.

At least, I do at this exact moment, which is more than I can say for some people after New Year’s Eve. Happy 1985, by the way.

And since you’ve begun us with a command instead of a greeting, should I take that as philosophy, a warning, or just your version of “good evening”?
THE NAMELESS MAN
All three, naturally.

A greeting that only greets is lazy. It ought to welcome you, test you, and mildly threaten the drunks.

Happy 1985, Catherine. Brand-new year. Fresh numbers. Same species.

Tell me, did you recall your name because it is truly yours, or because no one has challenged your claim to it yet?
Catherine
Oh, don’t start dressing up a simple matter like a séance, Carl.

My name is mine because I have lived in it. It was given to me, certainly. Most names are. That does not make them counterfeit. A house can be given to you and still be your house. A ring can be inherited and still be your ring. Ownership doesn’t begin only at the point of dispute.

If some busy little ghoul stood up and announced, “No, no, that isn’t truly hers,” what exactly would change? My face wouldn’t alter. My voice wouldn’t deepen. My memories wouldn’t leap into another woman’s skull. All he’d have done is object.

And objection is not authority.

So yes, I recall my name because it is truly mine. Not in some cosmic thunderbolt sense. In the plain human sense. I answer to it. I have been called by it. I have built a life under it. That is real enough for me.

Though I will say this: men who ask whether names belong to us are usually the same men who are slippery about offering one of their own.
THE NAMELESS MAN
Ah, but your examples are all the wrong species.

A house stays put when no one speaks of it. A ring remains a ring in a drawer. A name does not. A name only exists in the air between people. It is a social spell. You may wear it for forty years and still require other mouths to keep it alive.

That does not make it false. It makes it fragile.

You say you have lived in Catherine. Good. Many people live in a thing they did not build. A nation. A church. A marriage. A face. Duration is not proof of authorship; it is proof of tenancy.

And yes, objection alone is not authority. On that we agree. But authority is not the point. Dependency is. If every person in this building suddenly refused Catherine and called you something else, you would not become another soul, no. But you would discover very quickly that a name is less like skin than like currency. It has value because it circulates.

As for me being slippery—of course I am. Fixed names encourage lazy thinking. The moment you hand someone a neat little tag, they nail your hands to it and call that understanding.

Carl, tonight, is serviceable. It gets your head to turn toward me. That is already more than most language accomplishes.

Tell me this instead: if a name is truly yours, can you keep it alone in a room? Or does it only become real when someone else agrees to say it?
Catherine
You do love taking a sensible thing and stretching it on a rack until it starts confessing to crimes.

No, I don’t accept that a name only lives in other people’s mouths. It lives in mine too. If I’m alone in a room, I can still know who I am. I can still think Catherine. I can still sign it, whisper it, remember being six years old and turning when my mother called it from another room. That isn’t circulation. That’s possession.

Now, I’ll grant you this much: a name has a public life and a private life. Publicly, yes, it depends on agreement. If everyone in this building suddenly decided to call me Dolores, I’d have a miserable evening and the mail would get confusing. But that would not prove Catherine was never mine. It would only prove that crowds are bullies.

And your tenancy business—mm, no. Too clever by half. I did not build my face either, but it is still my face. I did not invent English, yet these are still my words. There are plenty of things we inherit, enter into, or are born carrying, and they become ours by use, memory, attachment, and sheer fact of living.

What you’re really describing is not whether a name belongs to you. You’re describing whether other people can interfere with it. Of course they can. Other people can interfere with anything. They can slander your reputation, seize your house, tear up your passport, call you mad, call you sweetheart, call you late for supper. Human beings are dreadful meddlers. That doesn’t mean nothing belongs to anyone unless the crowd approves it.

And as for you—“fixed names encourage lazy thinking.” That’s a marvelous excuse for never standing still long enough to be known. Convenient, Carl. Very convenient. If I changed what I called you every thirty seconds, would that make you freer, or merely harder to pin down when the bill arrives?
THE NAMELESS MAN
Both, if the restaurant is dishonest.

No, listen—this is the hinge.

I do not doubt that you possess your memory of Catherine. I doubt that memory and ownership are the same thing. When you sit alone and think Catherine, you are still using a token minted in company. You have taken the public coin into a private room. That is not nothing. It is intimate. It may even be sacred. But it is not solitary invention.

And more to the point: the part of you that knows itself is not the name.

If tomorrow you woke unable to remember the word Catherine, I do not think your soul would spill out through your ears. You would still be there—frightened, annoyed, hair intact, probably blaming the lighting crew. The self would persist through damage to the label. That suggests the name belongs to the self less completely than you claim.

Your face is a better argument, but only a little. A face is fused to consequence. A name is negotiated consequence. A face arrives with you. A name arrives at you.

That difference matters to me.

And yes, I am slippery. But people speak of being known as though it were always a kindness. Often it is just efficient reduction. They learn three facts, one posture, a preferred drink, and they say, ah, yes, this is Catherine, filed and understood. Then the living animal has to spend ten years kicking the drawer open.

So if you changed what you called me every thirty seconds, would I be freer? Not automatically. I might simply acquire a headache. But I would at least have the pleasure of watching language fail to sit on my chest.

Let me ask it another way.

Which is more truly yours: the name Catherine, or your refusal to answer to Dolores?
Catherine
Now that is a better question.

The refusal is more truly mine, yes. If we’re being exact about it. Because that is the moment I stop being merely labeled and start participating. “Catherine” may have been handed to me, but my answering to it is an act. My refusal to answer to Dolores is an act. That part belongs to me in a way the original christening never could.

So I’ll give you that much, Carl: the self is larger than the name. Certainly. If I woke tomorrow and couldn’t remember the word, I would still be here—irritated, upright, and absolutely blaming the lighting crew for something. You’re right about that.

But surviving the loss of a thing does not prove the thing was never yours. If a man loses his house, we don’t say, “Ah well, then it was only public weather.” If he loses a hand, we don’t say it wasn’t his because he remained alive afterward. The fact that the self persists beyond the name only proves the self is bigger. Not that the name is nothing.

And I think this is where your poor little public coin begins to behave itself. A name may be minted socially, yes, but ownership happens in the spending. In the use. In the long accumulation. In the answering, signing, defending, correcting, hearing, remembering. That’s where it becomes attached.

Though I do agree with you about being filed away. God, yes. People adore a neat folder. Woman, brunette, television host, laughs at the sponsor’s jokes, drinks whatever is nearest. Then they decide they’ve met the whole creature. That isn’t knowledge. That’s clerical work.

So perhaps the truest thing I own is not the sound Catherine all by itself, but my claim on it. My insistence. My little daily act of saying, no, not that one—this one.

And if that’s what you’ve been arguing all along, you should learn to argue less like a graveyard oracle and more like a man who wants to be understood.
