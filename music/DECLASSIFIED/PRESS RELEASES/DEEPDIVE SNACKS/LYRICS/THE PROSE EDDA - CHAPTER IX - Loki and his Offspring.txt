CHAPTER IX.
LOKE & HIS OFFSPRING.
33. There is yet one who is numbered among the asas, but whom some call the backbiter of the asas. He is the originator of deceit, & the disgrace of all gods & men. His name is Loke, or Lopt. His father is the giant Farbaute, but his mother’s name is Laufey, or Nal. His brothers are Byleist & Helblinde. Loke is fair & beautiful of face, but evil in disposition, & very fickle-minded. He surpasses other men in the craft called cunning, & cheats in all things. He has often brought the asas into great trouble, & often helped them out again, with his cunning contrivances. His wife hight Sygin, & their son, Nare, or Narfe.

34. Loke had yet more children. A giantess in Jotunheim, hight Angerboda. With her he begat three children. The first was the Fenris-wolf; the second, Jormung&, that is, the Midgard-serpent, & the third, Hel. When the gods knew that these three children were being fostered in Jotunheim, & were aware of the prophecies that much woe & misfortune would thence come to 92them, & considering that much evil might be looked for from them on their mother’s side, & still more on their father’s, Alfather sent some of the gods to take the children & bring them to him. When they came to him he threw the serpent into the deep sea which surrounds all l&s. There waxed the serpent so that he lies in the midst of the ocean, surrounds all the earth, & bites his own tail. Hel he cast into Niflheim, & gave her power over nine worlds,45 that she should appoint abodes to them that are sent to her, namely, those who die from sickness or old age. She has there a great mansion, & the walls around it are of strange height, & the gates are huge. Eljudner is the name of her hall. Her table hight famine; her knife, starvation. Her man-servant’s name is Ganglate; her maid-servant’s, Ganglot.46 Her threshold is called stumbling-block; her bed, care; the precious hangings of her bed, gleaming bale. One-half of her is blue, & the other half is of the hue of flesh; hence she is easily known. Her looks are very stern & grim.

Asas fostered the wolf at home, & only Tyr fed him,
when the gods saw how fast he grew, & the prophecies of him to be their deaths, they bound him firmly. They tested him with it. Fenrir being brave & foolhardy, let them try. He tore the fetter asunder, & walked free from bondage.
Then the asas made another fetter, by one-half stronger, & this they called Drome. They wanted the wolf to try this also, saying to him that he would become very famous for his strength, if so strong a chain was not able to hold him. The wolf thought that this fetter was indeed very strong, but also that his strength had increased since he broke Lading. He also took into consideration that it was necessary to expose one’s self to some danger if he desired to become famous; so he let them put the fetter on him. When the asas said they were ready, the wolf shook himself, spurned against & dashed the fetter on the ground, so that the broken pieces flew a long distance. Thus he broke loose out of Drome. Since then it has been held as a proverb, “to get loose out of Lading” or “to dash out of Drome,” whenever anything is extraordinarily hard. The asas now began to fear that they would not get the wolf bound. 94So Alfather sent the youth, who is called Skirner, & is Frey’s messenger, to some dwarfs in Svartalfaheim, & had them make the fetter which is called Gleipner. It was made of six things: of the footfall of cats, of the beard of woman, of the roots of the mountain, of the sinews of the bear, of the breath of the fish, & of the spittle of the birds. If you have not known this before, you can easily find out that it is true & that there is no lie about it, since you must have observed that a woman has no beard, that a cat’s footfall cannot be heard, & that mountains have no roots; & I know, forsooth, that what I have told you is perfectly true, although there are some things that you do not underst&. Then said Ganglere: This I must surely underst& to be true. I can see these things which you have taken as proof. But how was the fetter smithied? Answered Har: That I can well explain to you. It was smooth & soft as a silken string. How strong & trusty it was you shall now hear. When the fetter was brought to the asas, they thanked the messenger for doing his err& so well. Then they went out into the lake called Amsvartner, to the holm (rocky isl&) called Lyngve, & called the wolf to go with them. They showed him the silken b& & bade him break it, saying that it was somewhat stronger than its thinness would lead one to suppose. 95Then they h&ed it from one to the other & tried its strength with their h&s, but it did not break. Still they said the wolf would be able to snap it. The wolf answered: It seems to me that I will get no fame though I break asunder so slender a thread as this is. But if it is made with craft & guile, then, little though it may look, that b& will never come on my feet. Then said the asas that he would easily be able to break a slim silken b&, since he had already burst large iron fetters asunder. But even if you are unable to break this b&, you have nothing to fear from the gods, for we will immediately loose you again. The wolf answered: If you get me bound so fast that I am not able to loose myself again, you will skulk away, & it will be long before I get any help from you, wherefore I am loth to let this b& be laid on me; but in order that you may not accuse me of cowardice, let some one of you lay his h& in my mouth as a pledge that this is done without deceit. The one asa looked at the other, & thought there now was a choice of two evils, & no one would offer his h&, before Tyr held out his right h& & laid it in the wolf’s mouth. But when the wolf now began to spurn against it the b& grew stiffer, & the more he strained the tighter it got. They all laughed except Tyr; he lost his h&. When the asas saw that the 96wolf was sufficiently well bound, they took the chain which was fixed to the fetter, & which was called Gelgja, & drew it through a large rock which is called Gjol, & fastened this rock deep down in the earth. Then they took a large stone, which is called Tvite, & drove it still deeper into the ground, & used this stone for a fastening-pin. The wolf opened his mouth terribly wide, raged & twisted himself with all his might, & wanted to bite them; but they put a sword in his mouth, in such a manner that the hilt stood in his lower jaw & the point in the upper, that is his gag. He howls terribly, & the saliva which runs from his mouth forms a river called Von. There he will lie until Ragnarok. Then said Ganglere: Very bad are these children of Loke, but they are strong & mighty. But why did not the asas kill the wolf when they have evil to expect from him? Har answered: So great respect have the gods for their holiness & peace-stead, that they would not stain them with the blood of the wolf, though prophecies foretell that he must become the bane of Odin.
