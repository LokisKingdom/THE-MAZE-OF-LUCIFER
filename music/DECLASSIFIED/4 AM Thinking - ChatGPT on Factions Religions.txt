Not because the world changes—that's been done before—but because the player can't keep the world clean.

What you're describing is almost the opposite.

The one thing I'd be cautious about is making these changes literally irreversible in the sense that a player can accidentally ruin a save without warning. Some players enjoy that level of commitment, while others will feel trapped.

A middle ground could preserve the feeling without taking away agency:

That way the player still lives with the consequences of opening the door, but they can choose how to deal with the world they've created instead of simply wishing they'd never opened it. That kind of permanence tends to make choices feel meaningful without making experimentation feel like a mistake.

I actually think you've hit on the thing that separates this from a conventional RPG.

You're not designing factions.

You're designing self-contained mythologies.

A faction is usually a collection of NPCs with merchants, quests, reputation, and a headquarters.

What you're describing is closer to an entire expansion pack condensed into one thread of the game.

For the Moon God, the player doesn't just "join."

They slowly learn an entirely different language of interaction with the world.

They discover bottles have hidden uses.
Altars begin appearing.
Sacrifice becomes a game mechanic.
New dimensions open.
The enemies obey different rules.
The boss isn't simply stronger—it belongs to a different cosmology.
The ending changes the world permanently.

That's much bigger than "Faction #4."

I also wouldn't be afraid of letting one god have vastly more authored content than another.

Games often chase symmetry:

Reality doesn't have to work that way.

One religion might have:

six quests,

Another could be a sprawling, multi-hour descent through four dimensions, culminating in one of the game's most elaborate encounters.

As long as both are satisfying in their own way, players generally accept that different paths have different scopes.

The other thing I like is that you're tying narrative to mechanics instead of treating lore as collectible text.

A bottle mechanic isn't just a puzzle item.

It's a theological statement.

The Moon Cult believes people can be contained.

The player physically experiences that belief.

Likewise, if another religion worshipped memory, maybe books don't just explain the lore—they literally rewrite the world or alter the player's recollection of past events.

The belief system changes how the game behaves.

That final example—the tree that speaks nonsense and drops apples—is a good illustration of why these shouldn't all trend toward darkness. If every god leads to blood rituals and cosmic horror, the player begins to predict the tone. But if one path is unsettling because it is inscrutable rather than violent, another is comforting, another is transactional, another is tragic, and another is ecstatic, then each religion becomes memorable for a different reason.

The player isn't choosing between nine color-coded factions.

They're choosing between nine different philosophies made tangible—each with its own mechanics, stories, places, and consequences. If you can make each god feel like entering a different mythology with its own rules of reality, then discovering a new cult won't feel like finding another quest hub. It'll feel like opening an entirely different game hidden inside the first one.
