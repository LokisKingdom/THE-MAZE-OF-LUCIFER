PART I — WRITTEN SUBMISSIONS OF THE APPLICANT

HRTO FILE: 2026-67265-I Brendan S. Rose v Soundcloud 2026-08-17 4:12 PM

A. Overview

1.  The Applicant submits that this Application has a reasonable
    prospect of success and should proceed to a hearing on its merits.

2.  At its core, this is a case about equality of access to economic
    participation in a modern digital music service. The Applicant is an
    Ontario artist with a disability who uses artificial intelligence as
    a disability-related aid in the creation and production of music.

3.  The Applicant alleges that SoundCloud applies a rule or practice
    that prevents or restricts monetization of music because it was
    created using artificial intelligence. Whatever the purpose of that
    rule may be, its effect upon the Applicant is not neutral.

4.  The Applicant’s disability creates substantial barriers to
    conventional methods of professional music production. Traditional
    production ordinarily requires sustained interpersonal collaboration
    with musicians, bands, producers and others. Those methods have
    repeatedly proven inaccessible or ineffective for the Applicant
    because of disability-related limitations affecting sustained
    interpersonal and professional relationships.

5.  Artificial intelligence removed that barrier. It enabled the
    Applicant to transform his writing, compositions, concepts and
    artistic direction into completed musical works without depending
    upon a conventional band structure that he has been unable to
    maintain.

6.  The Applicant puts the matter simply:

  I am a human artist.

7.  Artificial intelligence does not change that fact. The Applicant
    conceives the works, writes and directs his artistic output, selects
    what is produced and published, develops the corpus in which those
    works exist, and presents those works to an audience. AI is the
    instrument and disability-related aid through which the Applicant is
    able to participate meaningfully in music production.

8.  The Applicant therefore rejects any suggestion that accommodating
    his use of AI would improperly divert money away from “human
    artists.” The Applicant is himself the human artist seeking equal
    access to monetization. The distinction created by the challenged
    policy is not, in his circumstances, between human creativity and
    non-human creativity. It is between a human artist able to work
    through conventional production methods and a human artist who
    alleges that disability requires him to work through an alternative
    technological method.

B. Adverse-Effect Discrimination Based on Disability

9.  The Applicant does not need to establish that SoundCloud adopted its
    AI policy because it intended to discriminate against persons with
    disabilities. That is not the principal theory advanced here.

10. The Application concerns adverse-effect or constructive
    discrimination: a requirement that may appear neutral but allegedly
    produces unequal consequences when applied to the Applicant because
    of a characteristic protected by the Ontario Human Rights Code.

11. Section 11 of the Code addresses requirements, qualifications or
    factors that are neutral on their face but result in exclusion,
    restriction or preference affecting persons identified by prohibited
    grounds. Ontario human-rights guidance likewise recognizes that
    discrimination need not be intentional and that service providers
    have obligations concerning disability-related barriers. (Ontario
    Human Rights Commission)

    [Authority note: Ontario Human Rights Commission v. Simpsons-Sears Ltd. (O’Malley), 1985 CanLII 18 (SCC), and Moore v. British Columbia (Education), 2012 SCC 61, support the adverse-effect / meaningful-access framework. See Authorities A2 and A4.]

12. The Applicant’s position can therefore be expressed as a
    straightforward chain:

Disability → disability-related barrier to conventional music production
→ AI as a means of overcoming that barrier → successful participation in
music production → exclusion of AI-assisted works from monetization →
adverse economic effect.

13. Before the availability of generative music technology, the
    Applicant spent many years attempting to make music through methods
    that did not provide a viable route to independent professional
    participation. Among other things, he created vocals over existing
    copyrighted recordings because he could not independently produce
    the underlying musical performances and could not reliably obtain
    them through conventional collaboration.

14. Generative music technology fundamentally changed that situation.
    For the first time, the Applicant had an instrument capable of
    addressing the particular barrier preventing independent production.

15. The Applicant describes Suno in functional terms as “my guitar.” The
    analogy is not intended to suggest that the technology is literally
    equivalent to a guitar. It describes its role: it is the means
    through which the Applicant can translate human authorship, writing,
    direction and creative decisions into music without requiring a
    conventional collaborative structure that disability makes
    substantially inaccessible to him.

16. The resulting productivity is not evidence that accommodation is
    unnecessary. On the contrary, the Applicant submits that his
    productivity demonstrates that the accommodation works.

17. A person does not cease to have a disability-related need merely
    because the technology addressing that need enables the person to
    perform successfully.

18. Ontario human-rights principles recognize that accommodation exists
    to remove barriers so that people with Code-related needs can have
    equal opportunities, access and benefits. Accommodation can require
    adjustment of policies or rules rather than requiring the affected
    person simply to function through the conventional method. (Ontario
    Human Rights Commission)

    [Authority note: Meiorin, Grismer and VIA Rail explain that standards must be examined for accommodation and individual difference rather than treated as self-justifying merely because they are uniform. See Authorities A5, A6 and A7.]

19. The Applicant alleges that SoundCloud’s monetization practice
    produces the opposite result. He can use technology to overcome the
    disability-related barrier and participate creatively, but the
    resulting works are then excluded from meaningful economic
    participation because of the technological method that enabled him
    to produce them.

20. On the Applicant’s case, this is not a request for preferential
    royalties, guaranteed commercial success, or payment for work that
    would otherwise be ineligible for legitimate reasons.

21. It is a request for an opportunity to participate in the same
    monetization system available to other eligible artists, without
    being excluded merely because disability-related needs resulted in
    the use of AI in producing the work.

22. The Applicant has generated millions of listens. The precise
    economic loss resulting from the alleged exclusion will ultimately
    depend upon evidence concerning SoundCloud’s monetization system,
    the streams that would otherwise have qualified, applicable royalty
    calculations, and other relevant factors.

23. The exact quantum need not be exaggerated to establish the alleged
    adverse effect. The essential allegation is that substantial
    listening activity occurred while the Applicant was denied the
    economic opportunity ordinarily associated with eligible monetized
    listening.

C. Creed and the Religious Corpus

24. The Application independently raises discrimination based upon
    creed.

25. The Applicant’s music forms part of a sincerely held religious
    corpus. The fact that the works may outwardly resemble rap,
    electronic music, popular music or other secular artistic forms does
    not determine their religious significance to the Applicant.

26. The Applicant’s creed is unconventional and has evolved over time.
    Neither fact, by itself, establishes insincerity.

27. The Applicant relies upon Syndicat Northcrest v. Amselem for the
    principle that the assessment of religious belief focuses upon the
    individual’s sincerely held belief rather than requiring the
    claimant to establish that the practice is objectively mandatory
    according to an external religious authority. The jurisprudence
    cautions against turning courts into arbiters of religious
    orthodoxy. (SCC CSC Lexum)

    [Authority note: Syndicat Northcrest v. Amselem, 2004 SCC 47, is the principal Supreme Court authority relied upon for sincerity and the Court’s refusal to make religious orthodoxy the legal test. See Authority A8.]

28. Ontario human-rights guidance similarly recognizes that a sincerely
    held creed belief need not be an essential element of a creed or
    recognized as valid by religious officials. (Ontario Human Rights
    Commission)

29. There is substantial evidence available concerning sincerity in this
    case. The Applicant has created an extraordinarily large corpus of
    works over an extended period and has consistently attributed
    profound religious significance to the creation and preservation of
    that corpus.

30. The Applicant’s historical conduct also includes extraordinary
    personal sacrifices associated with continuing this work under
    extremely adverse circumstances.

31. The Applicant does not contend that such sacrifice is required to
    establish religious sincerity. It plainly is not.

32. Rather, that history is evidence available in this particular case
    supporting the proposition that the Applicant’s characterization of
    the corpus as religious is sincerely held and is not a belief
    manufactured for purposes of litigation.

33. The Applicant further submits that where unusually substantial
    evidence of sincerity exists, that evidence cannot rationally be
    discounted merely because the particular creed or its means of
    artistic expression appears strange or unfamiliar to an outside
    observer. To do so would effectively subject an unconventional creed
    to a more demanding sincerity standard than a familiar creed.

34. That latter proposition is advanced as the Applicant’s argument. It
    does not depend upon claiming that Amselem expressly formulated that
    precise evidentiary rule.

35. Ontario’s adverse-effect framework is particularly relevant to this
    aspect of the case. The OHRC expressly recognizes that an
    organizational rule may constitute creed discrimination through its
    unintended effect and that accommodation seeks to permit people with
    different sincerely held creed beliefs to participate equally in
    services. (Ontario Human Rights Commission)

36. The Applicant alleges that the challenged monetization rule
    adversely affects the very works through which he creates and
    disseminates his religious corpus.

37. Accordingly, the disability and creed allegations are distinct but
    intersect:

The Applicant uses AI because of disability-related needs.

The Applicant uses that means of production to create works forming part
of his religious corpus.

The challenged monetization rule allegedly attaches an economic
disadvantage to those works because of that means of production.

38. One apparently neutral rule therefore allegedly produces adverse
    effects engaging two protected characteristics in the Applicant’s
    particular circumstances: disability and creed.

D. This Is an Equality Claim, Not an Attack on SoundCloud

39. The Applicant does not allege that SoundCloud is inherently hostile
    to persons with disabilities or to his creed.

40. Nor is this proceeding motivated by personal hostility toward
    SoundCloud.

41. The Applicant regards SoundCloud as a valuable service. It has
    enabled his music to reach listeners and has facilitated
    interactions with an audience that would otherwise have been
    difficult or impossible for him to develop.

42. The Applicant’s objection is narrower: he believes the monetization
    policy is wrong because, as applied to his circumstances, it permits
    him to contribute music, audience engagement and listening activity
    to the platform while preventing him from participating equally in
    the corresponding economic opportunity.

43. The remedy sought should therefore be understood principally as an
    equality remedy. The Applicant wants the opportunity to work,
    compete and receive monetization without a disability-related means
    of production automatically disqualifying his work.

44. Human-rights accommodation is not a demand for special privilege.
    Its purpose is substantive equality: addressing rules or structures
    that may function adequately for most people while creating barriers
    for people protected by the Code. (Ontario Human Rights Commission)

45. Again:

  I am a human artist.

46. The Applicant does not accept that recognizing his
    disability-related use of AI takes an opportunity away from human
    artists. Accommodating the Applicant would provide an opportunity to
    a human artist who alleges that conventional production methods are
    inaccessible because of disability.

E. Reasonable Prospect of Success

47. The Tribunal is not presently being asked to determine every
    disputed fact or finally quantify the Applicant’s damages.

48. The immediate question is whether the Application should be
    terminated at the summary stage notwithstanding the factual and
    legal foundation available to the Applicant.

49. The Applicant can identify evidence concerning his disability; the
    functional barriers affecting conventional music production; his
    historical attempts to make music before generative technology
    became available; his use of AI in overcoming those barriers; his
    extensive musical output; SoundCloud’s treatment of AI-assisted
    works; the listening activity generated by those works; the
    religious corpus and its development; and the sincerity of the
    beliefs associated with that corpus.

50. There are consequently genuine factual and legal questions
    concerning whether the challenged policy produces adverse-effect
    discrimination and, if so, whether the Applicant’s disability- and
    creed-related needs could be accommodated without undue hardship.

51. Those questions warrant determination on a proper evidentiary record
    rather than dismissal on the premise that AI-assisted music
    necessarily represents something other than human artistic
    participation.

52. The Applicant respectfully submits that the Application therefore
    has a reasonable prospect of success and should be permitted to
    proceed.

INTERVIEW SUMMARY
======================================================================

The answered interview establishes a concrete Ontario-centred factual theory. The Applicant says he lived in Ontario throughout the relevant period; created, uploaded and administered the works from Ontario; opened and operated the relevant SoundCloud account from Ontario; paid for SoundCloud's Artist Pro service from an Ontario bank account using Ontario billing information; accepted the relevant online terms while in Ontario; received SoundCloud's notices while in Ontario; and would have received monetization revenue in Ontario. He says SoundCloud knew through ordinary account and payment information that it was dealing with a Canadian/Ontario customer. He also identifies contemporaneous email chains capable of documenting the monetization/distribution discussions. These facts, if established, are more substantial than the bare proposition that an Ontario resident happened to suffer disappointment from a foreign website.

The interview also clarifies an important feature of the adverse-effect theory: the Applicant says he did not knowingly submit AI tracks for monetization because SoundCloud Support had informed him that AI monetization and distribution were prohibited by the governing rules. The allegation is therefore not that a conventional monetization application was formally filed and individually refused; it is that a generally applicable rule allegedly made the Applicant's disability-related production method economically ineligible. That distinction must remain explicit. It is also why the precise policy, its date, its terms, the Applicant's subscription and rights documentation, and the relevant support communications are more important than rhetorical estimates about what SoundCloud "would have" done.

The remaining unanswered questions in the earlier design document can be condensed into four evidentiary tasks rather than reproduced as hundreds of interrogatories: (1) identify the exact SoundCloud entity and contractual terms governing the Ontario account; (2) establish the precise AI monetization/distribution rule actually applied during the material period and whether accommodation was requested or reasonably available; (3) document the economic mechanism and any claimed loss using SoundCloud-specific royalty evidence rather than a generic per-stream estimate; and (4) identify the strongest authority on territorial jurisdiction over a foreign online service provider, including the effect, if any, of a foreign governing-law or forum-selection clause. These are matters for the next evidentiary stage of preparation, not reasons to dilute the present argument.

======================================================================
PART II — JURISDICTION: THE ACTUAL QUESTION IS THE LOCATION OF THE
ALLEGED DISCRIMINATORY RELATIONSHIP, NOT THE LOCATION OF A SERVER
======================================================================

53. The jurisdiction issue should be stated narrowly. The Applicant does not submit that Ontario acquires authority over every aspect of a multinational corporation merely because an Ontario resident opens a browser. Nor does he ask the Tribunal to regulate SoundCloud's worldwide business. The submission is that the particular service and contractual relationship challenged here was entered into, performed and experienced in Ontario, and that the alleged adverse effect of the impugned rule operated upon an Ontario artist in Ontario.

54. That distinction matters because the HRTO's current Practice Direction on Jurisdiction identifies applications whose events and allegations are "not sufficiently connected to Ontario" as potentially outside the Tribunal's jurisdiction. [J1] The present case, on the Applicant's pleaded facts, is not devoid of Ontario connections. It is saturated with them.

55. The Applicant says: he was resident in Ontario; the works were created in Ontario; the works were uploaded and administered in Ontario; the Artist Pro service was purchased and used from Ontario; payment was made from an Ontario bank account associated with an Ontario billing address; the relevant online terms were accepted from Ontario; SoundCloud communicated its decisions into Ontario; the alleged prohibition operated on the Applicant's Ontario catalogue; and the foregone economic benefit would have been received in Ontario.

56. The Code itself protects equal treatment in "services, goods and facilities" and separately protects the right to contract on equal terms, including on the grounds of creed and disability. Section 11 expressly addresses constructive discrimination arising from a requirement, qualification or factor that appears neutral but has an exclusionary or restrictive effect connected to a prohibited ground. [S1] That statutory architecture fits the Applicant's pleaded characterization better than an employment metaphor does. The strongest legal description of the relationship, on the present record, is service plus contract.

57. The Applicant therefore need not prove that a SoundCloud employee physically clicked a button in Toronto. The better territorial question is whether the allegedly discriminatory condition governed an Ontario-facing service and contractual relationship and produced its legally relevant adverse effect here.

58. No Supreme Court case located in this review directly decides the territorial reach of the Ontario Human Rights Code against a foreign online music platform. That absence must be stated rather than disguised. Two Supreme Court authorities are nonetheless highly instructive by analogy.

59. First, Club Resorts Ltd. v. Van Breda recognizes, in the civil jurisdiction context, objective connecting factors capable of grounding Ontario jurisdiction, including carrying on business in Ontario and a contract connected with the dispute made in Ontario. [A10] Van Breda is not an HRTO case and should not be presented as though its presumptive connecting factors mechanically decide this Tribunal's statutory jurisdiction. Its relevance is conceptual: Canadian jurisdiction doctrine takes seriously the place where a connected contract is made and performed, rather than reducing territorial analysis to the defendant's head-office address.

60. Second, Douez v. Facebook, Inc. is unusually close at the level of legal architecture. A Canadian user sued a foreign online platform whose standard-form terms selected a foreign forum and law. The Supreme Court did not treat the platform's boilerplate as the end of the inquiry. The plurality emphasized public-policy concerns, inequality of bargaining power in online consumer contracts, and the local statutory rights at stake. [A11] Douez concerned British Columbia privacy legislation and a court action, not the Ontario Human Rights Code or the HRTO. It is therefore persuasive by analogy, not controlling. But it refutes the overbroad intuition that a multinational digital platform can invariably answer a locally protected statutory claim simply by pointing to foreign headquarters and standard-form terms.

61. Renaud supplies a related human-rights principle. In the accommodation context, the Supreme Court rejected the proposition that private contractual arrangements could be used to contract out of human-rights obligations. [A9] Again, Renaud was an employment case involving a collective agreement. The Applicant does not invoke it as a magic formula invalidating every term in SoundCloud's TOS. He invokes the narrower proposition that private agreement does not, merely by existing, supersede mandatory human-rights obligations that otherwise apply.

62. The Applicant's limiting principle is therefore intelligible and modest:

    Ontario need only regulate the Ontario component of the relationship before it.

63. If SoundCloud offers a paid artist service to an Ontario resident, accepts payment from Ontario, permits that resident to upload and administer a catalogue from Ontario, offers monetization as part of that artist-service ecosystem, and applies a term that allegedly produces a Code-related adverse effect upon that Ontario user, the fact that SoundCloud is foreign does not make the Ontario nexus imaginary. Whether that nexus is legally sufficient is the jurisdiction question the Tribunal should decide on the facts and law, not by treating the corporate address as dispositive.

======================================================================
PART III — THE RULE 19A QUESTION: THIS CASE NEED NOT BE FINALLY PROVED
AT THE SUMMARY HEARING
======================================================================

64. The Registrar's August 17, 2026 letter defines the present task with unusual precision. The summary hearing is not the merits trial. The Tribunal may dismiss all or part of the Application if there is no reasonable prospect of success, and it specifically directs attention to two questions: how the Respondent's actions were linked to the Applicant's Code-protected characteristics, and what evidence the Applicant expects to be able to present at a merits hearing to prove the allegations on a balance of probabilities. [P1]

65. The Registrar also reproduces the reasoning in Dabic v. Windsor Police Service, 2010 HRTO 1994. Dabic recognizes two related modes of summary analysis. Some claims fail as a matter of law even if the alleged facts are assumed true. Others turn on whether evidence the applicant has, or that is reasonably available, could establish the required connection between the adverse event and the protected ground. [A1]

66. The Registrar's letter goes further: at the summary hearing the parties do not ordinarily call witnesses or adduce evidence; the adjudicator considers the materials already filed and the parties' submissions. The Applicant's version of events is presumed true unless contradicted by clear or undisputed evidence, although the Tribunal need not accept the Applicant's beliefs or assumptions about why events occurred. [P1] That final qualification is important. It means the Applicant cannot replace nexus evidence with conviction. But neither must he possess, before disclosure, every internal record explaining SoundCloud's policy or decision-making.

67. This is therefore a poor case for summary extinction if the legal theory is cognizable and the evidence identified could rationally prove it. The Applicant identifies existing or reasonably obtainable evidence concerning: his disability; the disability-related barriers to conventional collaborative production; his historical methods of making music before generative tools; his use of Suno to overcome those barriers; SoundCloud account and subscription records; support communications concerning AI monetization and distribution; the Artist Pro / monetization / distribution terms; catalogue and play data; the religious corpus and its development; and the history relevant to creed sincerity.

68. At this stage, the identified evidence need not finally prove the entire merits case. The relevant question is whether it is capable, if accepted at a merits hearing, of proving the required elements. That is precisely the distinction Dabic and the Registrar's letter require the Tribunal to preserve. [A1] [P1]

PART IV — DISABILITY: THE ADVERSE EFFECT IS NOT THAT AI EXISTS; IT IS
THAT THE ALLEGED RULE PENALIZES THE METHOD THAT REMOVES THE BARRIER
======================================================================

69. The disability claim does not depend upon proof of discriminatory animus. The Applicant does not need to prove that SoundCloud dislikes disabled people, knew his diagnosis when the rule was created, or designed the policy with discriminatory intent. O'Malley remains the foundational Supreme Court authority: a rule honestly adopted and neutral in form may nevertheless discriminate because of its effect, and discriminatory intent is not required. [A2]

70. The modern prima facie framework asks whether the claimant possesses a protected characteristic, experienced an adverse impact in the relevant social area, and whether the protected characteristic was a factor in that adverse impact. Moore states the test in the service context; Bombardier and Stewart reiterate that the protected ground need not be the exclusive cause, but there must be a real evidentiary connection rather than speculation. [A4] [A13] [A14]

71. The Applicant's theory supplies that connection in functional rather than diagnostic terms:

    disability
    -> difficulty sustaining the collaborative structure ordinarily required
       for conventional professional music production
    -> AI as the tool that removes that barrier
    -> independent and demonstrably productive artistic participation
    -> an alleged rule making AI-assisted work ineligible for monetization
    -> economic exclusion attached to the very method by which the barrier
       was overcome.

72. The phrase "Suno is my guitar" is useful because it prevents the technology from swallowing the human being. The Applicant is not asking the Tribunal to decide whether a machine is an artist. He says that he is the artist, and that AI is the production instrument through which his human authorship, selection, direction and judgment become music.

73. "I am a human artist" is therefore not rhetorical decoration. It answers a category error. If the Respondent's justification is that AI restrictions protect "human artists" from diverted royalties, the Applicant's answer is that the proposed class of protected human artists includes him. The policy cannot be justified merely by defining the person seeking accommodation out of the human category.

74. Moore is particularly useful here because the Supreme Court describes accommodation in terms of meaningful access to the service actually offered. [A4] The question is not whether the Applicant can somehow make sounds by a different and substantially inaccessible route. It is whether the service can be made meaningfully available without discrimination connected to disability.

75. Meiorin and Grismer add a structural insight. Human-rights law does not require the individual to conform to a standard first and ask for mercy afterward; standards themselves must be examined for whether they incorporate difference to the point required by the applicable undue-hardship analysis. [A5] [A6] VIA Rail makes the same point forcefully in a disability-access setting: convenience, efficiency or the existence of a standardized system does not automatically establish undue hardship. [A7]

76. None of those cases determines that SoundCloud must monetize Suno tracks. They do something more disciplined: they prevent the inquiry from ending at "that is our rule." If prima facie adverse-effect discrimination is established, justification and accommodation must be examined on evidence.

77. That evidentiary question may also have changed over time. SoundCloud's current public Help Center states that monetization generally depends on ownership or licensing of the content, and another current page says SoundCloud supports "AI tracks" subject to rights verification while also stating that its terms prohibit generative AI in a distribution-rejection context. [SC1] [SC2] Those current materials should not be retroactively substituted for the policy allegedly communicated to the Applicant during the material period. Their value is narrower: they demonstrate why the exact dated policy and the Applicant's support emails matter, and why the technical or commercial feasibility of individualized rights review should be proved rather than assumed impossible.

======================================================================
PART V — CREED: AMSELEM DOES NOT REQUIRE THE MUSIC TO LOOK RELIGIOUS
TO AN OUTSIDER
======================================================================

78. The creed branch is independently justiciable and forms part of the alleged discriminatory effect. The Applicant says the musical corpus is part of his religion. The legal question is not whether a rap record resembles a hymn to a judge.

79. Amselem rejects an objective-orthodoxy test. The Supreme Court held that a claimant need not prove that a practice is objectively required by official religious doctrine or endorsed by religious authorities; the focus is the individual's sincerely held belief and its nexus with religion. [A8]

80. That is why the Applicant's extraordinary historical conduct matters. He does not argue that a person must nearly die, impoverish himself, or make thousands of works to prove religion. Such a test would itself be grotesque. He argues that, in his particular case, extreme and costly persistence is probative evidence that the claimed religious significance was not casually invented for litigation.


82. The disability and creed claims intersect without becoming identical. The Applicant says AI is disability-related because it permits him to produce independently; he also says the works produced through that method form part of a sincerely held religious corpus. A single neutral policy may therefore have more than one protected-ground consequence.

83. The creed issue also illustrates why the summary-hearing standard matters. An adjudicator may test sincerity, and the Tribunal need not simply accept a religious label. But Amselem makes the nature of that inquiry legally constrained: courts and tribunals are not to transform themselves into arbiters of religious dogma. [A8] The Applicant says he has extensive longitudinal evidence of belief, conduct, production and sacrifice from which sincerity could be assessed at a merits hearing.

======================================================================
PART VI — CONTRACT, ONLINE TERMS, AND A FOREIGN PLATFORM
======================================================================

84. The Applicant's interview states, in colloquial terms, that Canadian law supersedes SoundCloud's TOS. The legal proposition advanced here is narrower and more exact.

85. A standard-form contract can define private rights and obligations. It cannot be assumed, without analysis, to erase mandatory statutory protections that otherwise govern the relationship. Renaud is strong human-rights authority for the proposition that parties cannot contract out of human-rights obligations. [A9]

86. Douez supplies a particularly useful digital-platform analogy. The Supreme Court confronted Facebook's standard-form forum-selection clause in a dispute involving locally protected statutory rights. The plurality treated the nature of the right, public policy and the inequality inherent in consumer online contracting as relevant to whether the foreign forum clause should be enforced. [A11]

87. Uber Technologies Inc. v. Heller provides a second, Ontario-centred illustration of judicial scrutiny of inaccessible dispute-resolution terms in a standard-form digital-platform contract. The case turns on unconscionability and arbitration rather than human-rights jurisdiction, so it should be used sparingly. [A15] Its relevance is not that every foreign clause is invalid, but that "clicking agree" is not a universal answer to questions of mandatory law, access to adjudication, or enforceability.

88. The Applicant relies only upon contractual provisions that are established in the record. No foreign forum or governing-law term should be presumed in the absence of the governing agreement.

======================================================================
PART VII — DAMAGES, ECONOMIC LOSS, AND THE THIN-SKULL POINT
======================================================================


90. None of that defeats the allegation of adverse economic effect at the summary stage. The legal distinction is between the existence of an allegedly denied economic opportunity and the final quantum of damages. The former can be material even while the latter remains to be proved.

91. The interview also raises the thin-skull rule. Athey v. Leonati is authoritative Supreme Court tort law for the proposition that a wrongdoer generally takes the victim as found, including unusual vulnerability. [A12] But this proceeding is under the Human Rights Code, not a negligence action. Unless specific HRTO or appellate human-rights authority connects that tort doctrine to Code compensation, Athey should be treated as an analogy concerning vulnerability and causation, not as an independent rule the Tribunal is bound to apply to damages.


======================================================================
PART VIII — CONCLUSION
======================================================================

103. The architecture of this case is unusual because the technology is new. The governing legal principles are not.

104. Human-rights law has dealt for decades with rules that appear neutral until they encounter a person whose protected characteristics make the rule operate differently. O'Malley names the phenomenon. Moore supplies the modern prima facie framework and the language of meaningful access. Meiorin, Grismer and VIA Rail explain why a standard cannot immunize itself from scrutiny merely by being standardized. Amselem prevents unfamiliar religious expression from being judged according to external orthodoxy. Renaud prevents private agreement from becoming an automatic escape hatch from human-rights duties. Douez demonstrates, in the digital-platform context, why local statutory rights, public policy and standard-form foreign terms cannot be collapsed into the proposition that a multinational's boilerplate necessarily wins. [A2] [A4] [A5] [A6] [A7] [A8] [A9] [A11]

105. The Applicant's claim can therefore be stated without exaggeration.

106. He is a human artist with a disability. He says that conventional collaborative production creates disability-related barriers severe enough to exclude him from meaningful professional participation. He found a technological method that removes those barriers. He used it successfully. He then encountered an alleged monetization rule that attached economic ineligibility to the very method by which he had overcome the barrier.

107. That is a legally intelligible adverse-effect allegation. It may ultimately succeed or fail on evidence. What it is not is inherently incapable of proof.

108. The creed branch is similarly justiciable. The Applicant says the works constitute a sincerely held religious corpus. The Tribunal is entitled to assess sincerity. Amselem is equally clear that sincerity is not the same question as conventionality and that external religious orthodoxy is not the controlling test. [A8] The Applicant identifies a substantial history from which sincerity could be tested.

109. The territorial argument is likewise more than residence alone. The Applicant identifies an Ontario-based service and contractual relationship: Ontario creation, upload, account administration, payment, agreement, receipt of communications, alleged adverse effect and economic consequence. The HRTO's Practice Direction asks whether the events and allegations are sufficiently connected to Ontario. [J1] The Applicant says they are. Van Breda and Douez do not decide the HRTO question, but they demonstrate that Canadian law treats contract formation, local business relationships, statutory rights and digital-platform structure as legally significant connecting considerations rather than irrelevant scenery. [A10] [A11]

110. The Applicant does not ask the Tribunal to declare that every AI-generated track must be monetized, that copyright and licensing rules disappear, that SoundCloud must pay an invented fixed royalty per stream, or that a German corporation becomes subject to every Ontario rule in every country merely because it operates a website.

111. He asks for something narrower and more recognizable in human-rights law: that an Ontario service relationship not be insulated from examination where a facially neutral rule is alleged to make the disability-related means of participation economically worthless.

112. SoundCloud may ultimately establish that the Applicant's works were ineligible for reasons unrelated to disability or creed; that the alleged policy has been misunderstood or changed; that rights ownership or licensing requirements control the result; that the proposed accommodation would alter the essential nature of the service or cause undue hardship; that Ontario lacks sufficient territorial jurisdiction; or some combination of those propositions. Those are defences and merits questions. They are not presently established merely because they can be imagined.

113. Conversely, the Applicant cannot prevail merely by asserting sincerity, disability, loss, jurisdiction or causation. He must prove them at the appropriate stage. The significance of this submission is that he identifies a coherent legal route by which they can be proved, and identifies evidence that exists or is reasonably available to support that route.

114. That is enough to make the summary-hearing question concrete. The issue is not whether the Tribunal is already certain the Applicant will win. The issue is whether there is a reasonable prospect that the evidence could establish a Code violation. [A1] [P1]

115. The Applicant's position can be reduced to the sentence that has survived every layer of the argument:

    I am a human artist.

116. If AI is the Applicant's disability-related instrument, its success does not make the disability disappear. If thousands of unconventional musical works are, in his sincerely held belief, part of a religious corpus, their genre does not by itself make the belief fictitious. If a multinational service relationship is carried on with him in Ontario, the foreign location of the corporate headquarters does not by itself erase every Ontario connecting factor. Each proposition remains open to evidence and law.

117. The Applicant respectfully requests that the Tribunal decline to dismiss the Application at the summary stage, determine that the pleaded disability and creed theories have a reasonable prospect of success, resolve the territorial issue on the full Ontario nexus advanced here, and permit the matter to proceed to the next stage of the HRTO process.

======================================================================
FINAL WORD
======================================================================

The conclusion sought here is not that novelty defeats law. It is that old legal principles remain capable of examining new instruments. The Applicant does not ask the Tribunal to decide that artificial intelligence is human, religious, disabled, or entitled to anything. He asks the Tribunal to look through the instrument to the human being using it, and then to ask the ordinary human-rights question: what did the rule do to this person, in this service, because of characteristics the Code protects?

The Supreme Court's formulation in O'Malley remains an apt final instruction:

"The Code aims at the removal of discrimination."

— Ontario Human Rights Commission v. Simpsons-Sears Ltd. (O'Malley), 1985 CanLII 18 (SCC), [1985] 2 S.C.R. 536.
