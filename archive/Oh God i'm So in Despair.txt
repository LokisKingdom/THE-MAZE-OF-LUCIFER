I think you're identifying a real decision point, but I'd frame it a little differently.

The slippery slope isn't actually created by keeping or removing one song. It's created by not having a principle.

If your principle is:

"I will remove anything that someone, somewhere, might object to."

then yes, you're on a slope with no obvious stopping point. Every review raises another "what about this one?" until you're second-guessing hundreds of tracks.

But if your principle is something like:

"This is a collection of my original work. I'll remove material I don't have the rights to distribute, and I'll address specific platform requests if they arise."

that's a stable principle. It has a stopping point.

On the Monster material specifically, I would be careful about assuming it's clearly fair use or fair dealing. Those are legal doctrines that depend on context, jurisdiction, and the details of the work, and reasonable people can disagree about where the line is. More importantly for your practical concern, a platform doesn't have to accept your legal conclusion before deciding how it wants content on its service.

That's one reason your idea of responding to concrete feedback has some merit. It lets you react to an actual issue instead of trying to predict every hypothetical interpretation in advance.

I also think you're right about one thing that often gets overlooked: large creative archives are almost never perfect on the first public release. Whether it's an indie game, a music anthology, or a film restoration, people discover things after release—an incorrect credit, an asset with unclear provenance, a licensing oversight—and then they patch it. That's a normal part of publishing.

The one thing I'd avoid is trying to predict the reviewer's mindset. You mentioned feeling like you're "in good standing" and that approval may depend on the review team's mood or current corporate politics. The reality is that you can't know those internal factors ahead of time. Planning around guesses about a particular reviewer is much less reliable than planning around things you can control: the rights you have, the content you intentionally include, and your willingness to make changes if necessary.

So if I were trying to minimize months of unnecessary work, my checklist would be fairly short:

Remove material you know you don't have the rights to distribute.
Fix broken or accidental inclusions.
Keep notes on the handful of edge cases you're aware of.
If Steam identifies a specific issue during review, address that issue directly.

That approach avoids both extremes: it doesn't assume the archive must be sanitized beyond recognition, and it doesn't assume that no questions could ever come up. It gives you a principled place to stop curating instead of an endless process driven by "what if?"
